# 2. Method

**Overview.** This section explains how the code traces water and what each of its outputs means. A backward trace follows the water of a destination, such as a well, back to where it entered the model. A forward trace follows the water of a source, such as recharge, to where it leaves. Both work on the cell-by-cell flows that a MODFLOW 6 model saves, for one time step or for every step of a transient model. <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.1; src/gwsource/trace.py (backward, forward); src/gwsource/transient.py, src/gwsource/tracking.py --> The subsections build up from the two fractions that a trace computes to the volumes, checks and travel times that follow from them, and end with transient flow. The derivations and the solution of the equations are given in the Supporting Information ({SI: method in full}).

**Full mixing and the cell budget.** Every output of the code rests on the assumption of full mixing. Full mixing treats each active model cell as one well-mixed volume of water. All the water leaving a cell then has the same mix of origins and the same mix of fates, whichever outflow it takes. <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (full mixing); src/gwsource/assemble.py (outflow shares q_ij / O_i) --> The code reads the cell-to-cell flows (FLOW-JA-FACE; Langevin et al. 2017) and the source and sink terms from the MODFLOW 6 budget file. The flow from cell $i$ to its neighbour $j$ is $q_{ij} \ge 0$. A source term brings water into a cell, such as recharge or leakage from a river. A sink term takes water out, such as a well or a drain. Each term is kept separate by package and by boundary, so that a trace can follow the water of one well or one river reach. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) "The backward and forward fractions", paragraph 1; src/gwsource/assemble.py (term_totals, per-package terms) --> If $\mathrm{Src}_i$ and $\mathrm{Snk}_i$ are the sums of the source and sink terms of cell $i$, its total inflow, total outflow and cell budget error are

$$I_i = \sum_j q_{ji} + \mathrm{Src}_i, \qquad O_i = \sum_j q_{ij} + \mathrm{Snk}_i, \qquad r_i = I_i - O_i. \tag{1}$$

The budget of a MODFLOW model never balances exactly, because the solver stops once the heads meet a convergence tolerance. The cell budget error $r_i$ is therefore small but not zero. The code keeps this error in the equations below and reports it, but never forces it to zero. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/diagnostics.py (cell_residual, budget_error; checks include the residual term); docs/paper/terms.md (cell budget error: reported, never forced to zero) -->

**The backward fraction F.** The backward fraction answers the question of where the water of a sink comes from. The destination can be one well, one stream reach, or several wells treated together as one destination group. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/selectors.py (selection by package, boundary name, term, cells or zone); src/gwsource/transient.py GroupSpec (destination groups) --> The backward fraction $F_i$ is the share of the water leaving cell $i$ that later leaves the model through the destination. Of the water that leaves cell $i$ in one day, the part $d_i$ leaves through the destination directly. The part $q_{ij}$ flows to neighbour $j$. Because full mixing gives all the water leaving cell $j$ the same fate, a share $F_j$ of that part later reaches the destination. The volume of water leaving cell $i$ that ends at the destination is therefore

$$F_i\, O_i = \sum_j q_{ij}\, F_j + d_i . \tag{2}$$

Dividing by the outflow gives the fraction itself,

$$F_i = \sum_j \frac{q_{ij}}{O_i}\, F_j + \frac{d_i}{O_i}. \tag{3}$$

In words: a cell's share is the share that leaves directly through the destination plus the outflow-weighted mean of the shares of its downstream neighbours. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) Eqs. (S2) and (1); src/gwsource/assemble.py module docstring (gross accounting rows) -->

Fig. 1a works through an eight-cell example. Water enters from the west edge and as recharge. It leaves through a well and a river. The well cell receives 11 m³/d, of which its well pumps 6 m³/d. The other 5 m³/d flow on to the river without returning to the well. Eq. (3) therefore gives F = 6/11 = 0.55 in the well cell. <!-- evidence: display/E1.md Figure 1 caption (F = 6/11 = 0.55); studies/paper/method_figures.py GRAPH_EDGES, GRAPH_TERMS (cell 6: 9 + 1 in from neighbours, 1 recharge, so 11 in; 6 to the well, 5 on to cell 7, which drains to the river); studies/paper/gallery_figure.py make_method_single -->

Eq. (3) holds in every cell, so the fractions form one set of linear equations. One solve of this set gives the fractions to every destination group at once. <!-- evidence: si/B.md Eq. (S1) (each column of F holds one destination group); src/gwsource/solve.py (factorize, one factorisation for all right-hand sides) --> The code solves the cells in the order the water flows. A flow loop is a group of cells in which water circulates, as it can with the XT3D option of MODFLOW 6 (Provost et al. 2017). The code solves each loop as one small block, so the result is still the exact solution ({SI: method in full}). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/config.py (solver "auto": topological substitution if acyclic, block-triangular sparse LU otherwise); src/gwsource/solve.py module docstring; si/B.md "Solution of the linear systems" --> A map of F shows where a destination's water comes from. A contributing area is the set of cells where F exceeds a threshold that the user chooses, such as 0.5. The choice of threshold is a judgement (Section 6).

**The forward fraction G.** The forward fraction answers the opposite question, which is where the water of a source goes. The forward fraction $G_i$ is the share of the water entering cell $i$ that came from the source. Let $s_i$ be the inflow of cell $i$ through the source. All of $s_i$ came from the source by definition. Of the water that arrives from neighbour $j$, a share $G_j$ came from it. The volume of water entering cell $i$ that came from the source is therefore

$$G_i\, I_i = \sum_j q_{ji}\, G_j + s_i . \tag{4}$$

Eq. (4) mirrors Eq. (2), with inflows in place of outflows and the source in place of the destination. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) Eq. (S4); src/gwsource/assemble.py (forward rows G_i - sum_j (q_ji/I_i) G_j = sum_{k in S} q_k / I_i) --> A map of G shows how much of each cell's water came from the source (Fig. 2c). G equals the concentration that a transport model reaches at equilibrium when the source water carries a concentration of 1 and all other water a concentration of 0 (Table 1, row 03). <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §9, cell 24 (GWT at equilibrium equals G in every cell); docs/paper/terms.md (transient tracking checks: steady G vs GWT at equilibrium) -->

**Volumes and the water-budget checks.** A fraction says what share of a cell's water reaches the destination, but not how much water that is. A fraction times a flow gives a volume of water per unit time. The volume through cell $i$ is $F_i O_i$. It is the part of the water passing through the cell that later reaches the destination. The volume from cell $i$ is $F_i$ times the cell's inflow through its source terms. It is the water that enters the model in that cell and later reaches the destination. The volumes from all cells, added up package by package, split the destination's water by source (Fig. 2b). In the same way, the volumes of a forward trace split a source's water by the sink through which it leaves. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/metrics.py docstring (volume_through = F x O, gross; volume_from, volume_from_by_term; forward volume_through = G x I, volume_to, volume_to_by_term) -->

Each trace also checks these volumes against the flows the model saved. The closure check follows from adding Eq. (2) over all cells. It says that the source water traced to the destination, once corrected for the cell budget errors, adds up to the water the destination takes. The forward–backward check compares the backward and forward traces of one source and destination. The boundary-control check requires the fractions to all sinks to add up to 1 in every cell with outflow. Because all three checks include the cell budget errors, they hold exactly for any flow field ({SI: method in full}). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/diagnostics.py (backward_closure, forward_closure, duality, boundary control; "with the residual term the checks hold to round-off"); docs/paper/terms.md (closure, forward–backward and boundary-control checks); si/B.md Eqs. (S3)–(S5) --> A check error above round-off therefore points to a fault in the tracing. The checks test the solution of the equations. They cannot show that the flow model is right, or that full mixing describes how water moves in the aquifer. <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.5, last paragraph -->

**Gross and net accounting at weak sinks.** Section 1 introduced weak sinks. A weak sink is a cell whose sink terms take only part of the cell's outflow. At a weak sink the method needs a rule for how much of the cell's water to attribute to the sink. Eq. (2) applies gross accounting, which the code uses by default. Each sink term takes its share of the cell's total outflow, as it would in a transport model. Only that water counts as the sink's water. The rest passes on to the neighbouring cells. <!-- evidence: Potter et al. 2008 p. 336, Fig. 1; src/gwsource/config.py (accounting "gross" default); docs/paper/terms.md (gross accounting) -->

Under net accounting, all the water passing through a destination cell counts as the destination's water. The destination cells are then fixed at F = 1. This is how FlowSource attributes water at a well (Foley and Black 2013). In Fig. 1a, gross accounting gives the well the 6 m³/d it pumps. Net accounting adds the 5 m³/d that flow on through the well cell to the river, so the well is attributed all 11 m³/d. <!-- evidence: studies/paper/method_figures.py GRAPH_EDGES, GRAPH_TERMS (cell 6: 11 in; 6 to the well, 5 on); full draft Section 2.4; src/gwsource/config.py (net: destination cells absorbing, the reference method, for regression and comparison only) --> The difference matters most at a small well in a cell that carries much more water than the well pumps. In one such example (Table 1, row 02), net accounting attributes 6.7 times the pumping to the well. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 13 (weak_well 5 m³/d, net 33.667 m³/d, 6.7 times); draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.4 --> We would use net accounting to compare with a study made with FlowSource. Its details are given in {SI: method in full}. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/assemble.py module docstring (net accounting from the cell-to-cell flows; no residual term) -->

**Mean travel time.** Fractions say how much water reaches a destination, but not when it arrives. The water volume $V_i$ of cell $i$ is porosity times the saturated volume of the cell. Fractions need no porosity, but travel times do. Porosity is therefore a required input, with no default value. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/traveltime.py (travel_time: porosity required, no default; water_volume) --> The mean time that water stays in a fully mixed cell is its residence time $\tau_i = V_i/O_i$. Let $P_{ij} = q_{ij}/O_i$ be the share of the outflow of cell $i$ that goes to cell $j$. Let $m_i$ be the mean travel time from entering cell $i$ to leaving the model, counted only for water that leaves through the destination and weighted by $F_i$. Then

$$m_i = \tau_i\, F_i + \sum_j P_{ij}\, m_j . \tag{5}$$

Each cell adds its own residence time, weighted by its F. The sum then passes upstream with the outflow shares, in the same way as F in Eq. (3). The mean travel time of the water that reaches the destination from cell $i$ is $m_i/F_i$ (Fig. 2d). It is defined under gross accounting only, and only where F is above a small threshold. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/traveltime.py module docstring (m_i = E[R_i] P(D) + sum_j P_ij m_j; mean_time = m / F, NaN where F <= fraction_threshold; gross accounting only); si/B.md (round 1) Eq. (S10) --> The travel times have their own closure check,

$$\sum_i \mathrm{Src}_i\, m_i - \sum_i r_i\, m_i = \sum_i V_i F_i . \tag{6}$$

The right-hand side is the volume of water in the model that will end at the destination. The check says that this volume equals the destination's inflow times the flow-weighted mean travel time of all the water the destination takes. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/traveltime.py docstring, "Exact identity (backward)"; si/B.md (round 1) Eq. (S11) --> For this reason, agreement with another method in the mean travel time of all the water a sink takes is not an independent test when both use the same water volumes (example C in Section 4). <!-- evidence: draft_reduced/reviews/reduced_r1.md item 3 ("Both follow from the same water volume, so this agreement is not an independent test") --> A mean travel time also averages fast and slow water that have mixed in the cells. It is not a first-arrival time.

**Transient flow: snapshots and transient tracking.** In a transient model, the water that reaches a sink in one time step may have entered the aquifer in an earlier step. A snapshot ignores this. It traces each time step on its own flows, as if they were steady. Water released from storage counts as a source term. Water taken into storage counts as a sink term (Fig. 1b, top). This is how the published descriptions of FlowSource treat transient models (Section 1). A snapshot assumes that water reaches the sink in the same step it entered the aquifer. Recharge in the wet season then cannot reach a well in the dry season. A snapshot also cannot say where the water released from storage came from. <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.7, paragraph 1; draft_reduced/sections/01_introduction.md (Environment Agency 2019, Appendix C4.4); src/gwsource/transient.py (snapshots: storage release a source, storage gain a sink) -->

We extend full mixing through time. We refer to this as transient tracking. Water from the chosen sources is tagged, as a tracer would be. The tagged water is then carried from step to step through the water volume of each cell (Fig. 1b, bottom). Hydraulic mixing cells carry mixed water through storage in integrated surface water–groundwater models in a similar way (Partington et al. 2011). <!-- references/docs/text/2011_Partington_etal_hydraulic_mixing_cell.txt (volumetric balance of each flow component over each time step, from the previous step's storage); full draft Section 6 --> Storage terms are not traced as source or sink terms. Instead they change the water volume of a cell from $V_i^{\mathrm{old}}$ at the start of a step to $V_i^{\mathrm{new}}$ at its end. Let $c_i$ be the share of a cell's water that is tagged. Over step $n$, of length $\Delta t$,

$$V_i^{\mathrm{new}} c_i^n - V_i^{\mathrm{old}} c_i^{n-1} = \Delta t \Big[ \sum_j q_{ji}\, c_j^n + s_i^n - (O_i + r_i)\, c_i^n \Big], \tag{7}$$

where $O_i$ now leaves out the storage terms. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring, forward scheme, eq. (1) --> The tagged water in a cell at the end of a step is the tagged water at the start, plus what arrives from upstream cells and from the tagged source, less what leaves. The outflows carry the cell's mix at the end of the step, because the scheme is implicit in time, as in GWT (Langevin et al. 2022). The scheme has no dispersion term. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring ("transport is advective only (no dispersion), upstream weighted and implicit in time"); si/B.md "Transient tracking" --> The term $r_i c_i$ lets the cell budget error leave the cell with the cell's own share of tagged water. GWT treats the error in the same way, which Langevin et al. (2022) call the flow-imbalance correction. With this treatment, a run that tags all water gives c = 1 in every cell, as it should. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring (a = 1: FLOW_IMBALANCE_CORRECTION, Langevin et al. 2022 eq. 2-14/2-15; c = 1 solves (1) when every source and the initial water are tagged) -->

Transient tracking needs porosity and the budget of every time step (SAVE BUDGET ALL). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring and TrackingConfig (porosity required; "Every time step of the simulation must be in the budget file (OC BUDGET ALL)") --> The water in the model at the start of the simulation can be tagged as its own group. We refer to it as initial water. Initial water is not necessarily water from before pumping began, because a simulation need not start before pumping. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring (tagged initial water: c^0 = 1, no source); docs/paper/terms.md (initial water) --> The result also depends on the water volumes and on the length of the transport step. The two volume options and the transport sub-steps are described in {SI: method in full}. Example B (Section 4) reports their effect. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring (volumes "gwt" and "flow"; sub-steps) -->

**Backward tracking in time.** Forward tracking says how much of each cell's water came from a source. It cannot say which of the water now in the aquifer will reach a sink, or by when. Backward tracking in time answers this question. It solves the backward counterpart of Eq. (7), from the last step of the simulation back to the first. This differs from MODPATH backward tracking, in which particles start at the sink and move against the flow. Backward tracking in time is the discrete counterpart of the adjoint methods for continuous transport (Neupauer and Wilson 1999; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006). <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (backward tracking in time); src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring, backward (adjoint) scheme; si/B.md "Backward tracking in time" -->

The backward fraction in time $w_i^n$ is the share of the water in cell $i$ at the start of step $n$, or entering it during the step, that leaves through the destination before the simulation ends. One value describes both kinds of water, because the implicit scheme mixes them alike. Over step $n$,

$$\Big[ V_i^{\mathrm{new}} + \Delta t\, (O_i + r_i) \Big]\, w_i^n = \Delta t \Big[ \sum_j q_{ij}\, w_j^n + d_i^n \Big] + V_i^{\mathrm{old},\,n+1}\, w_i^{n+1}. \tag{8}$$

<!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring, eq. (2) (A^n)^T w^n = dt^n d^n + B^(n+1) w^(n+1), w^(N+1) = 0; step_matrix (A = diag(V^new + dt (O + a r)) - dt Q^T, so row i of A^T is (V^new_i + dt (O_i + r_i)) w_i - dt sum_j q_ij w_j); _backward_rhs (B^(n+1) = diag(V^old of step n+1)); written cell by cell here, in matrix form as Eq. (S8) --> The bracket on the left is the volume of all the water that was in the cell at the start of the step or entered it during the step. The right-hand side follows this water along the three routes that can bring it to the destination. It can flow to a neighbour $j$, from which a share $w_j^n$ reaches the destination. It can leave through the destination directly. It can also stay in the cell into the next step, where its share is $w_i^{n+1}$. After the last step no water can still reach the destination, so the solve starts there with w = 0.

A map of w at the start of the simulation is a time-limited contributing area, such as the share of each cell's water pumped within 30 years (Fig. 2f). The same backward run gives the share that leaves through other sinks and the share still stored in the model at the end. The three shares add up to 1, apart from a small remainder that the code reports. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring (fraction_through + fraction_to_other_sinks + fraction_in_storage_at_end + fraction_unaccounted = 1); display/E1.md Figure 2 caption (f), 30 years --> The transient forward–backward check requires the tagged water that the destination takes under forward tracking to equal the tagged inflow and initial water, each multiplied by its share w ({SI: method in full}). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring (duality identity (3), exact for any volumes and any a); src/gwsource/tracking.py tracking_duality --> When the flows are held constant for long enough, c tends to the steady-state forward fraction G.
