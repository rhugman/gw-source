# 2. Method

**Overview.** This section explains how the code traces water and what each of its outputs means. A backward trace follows the water of a destination, such as a well, back to where it entered the model. A forward trace follows the water of a source, such as recharge, to where it leaves. Both work on the cell-by-cell flows that a MODFLOW 6 model saves, for one time step or for every step of a transient model. <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.1; src/gwsource/trace.py (backward, forward); src/gwsource/transient.py, src/gwsource/tracking.py; sections/03_software.md (reads the binary grid file and the budget file) --> The backward fraction F maps where a sink's water comes from. A contributing area follows from it (example A in Section 4). The forward fraction G maps where a source's water goes. A trace also splits a sink's water by source, for example into recharge and river water. With a porosity, a trace also gives mean travel times (example C). For transient models, the code either traces each time step on its own flows or carries tagged water through storage (example B). <!-- evidence: structure review r2 finding 3 (map of outputs to examples A–C; the example letters are kept for the Section 4 rewrite); src/gwsource/traveltime.py and tracking.py (porosity required) --> The derivations and the solution of the equations are given in the Supporting Information ({SI: method in full}).

**Full mixing and the cell budget.** Every output of the code rests on the assumption of full mixing. Full mixing treats each active model cell as one well-mixed volume of water. All the water leaving a cell then has the same mix of origins and the same mix of fates, whichever outflow it takes. <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (full mixing); src/gwsource/assemble.py (outflow shares q_ij / O_i) --> At the edge of a contributing area, the water of a cell then splits between the sink and other outlets, where particles from the same cell would mostly either reach the sink or miss it. For this reason, areas traced under full mixing are wider and more diffuse than particle areas. Larger cells widen them further (Section 6). <!-- evidence: sections/04_examples.md "Sources and area" (in the 372 edge cells most particles either reach the well or miss it, full mixing gives an in-between value); sections/06_tradeoffs.md paragraph 1 (wider and more diffuse; halving the cell size narrows the band); docs/paper/terms.md (numerical dispersion scales with cell size) -->

The code reads the cell-to-cell flows (FLOW-JA-FACE; Langevin et al. 2017) and the source and sink terms from the MODFLOW 6 budget file. The flow from cell $i$ to its neighbour $j$ is $q_{ij} \ge 0$. Source terms, such as recharge or leakage from a river, bring water into a cell. Sink terms, such as wells or drains, take it out. Each term is kept separate by package and by boundary, so that a trace can follow the water of individual boundary conditions. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) "The backward and forward fractions", paragraph 1; src/gwsource/assemble.py (term_totals, per-package terms) --> A stream reach, a lake or a multi-aquifer well can stay a source or sink term of the aquifer cells it exchanges water with. It can also be traced as a cell of its own, from its package budget file, so that its flows to the aquifer, to other reaches and through the water mover (MVR) become flows $q_{ij}$ like any other. The cells of models linked by exchanges are treated as one set of cells. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/network.py module docstring (nodes: cells of every GWF model, then one node per reach, lake, well or UZF cell; edges: intercell flows, GWF-GWF exchanges, cell <-> package node from the aquifer-side record, package node <-> package node FLOW-JA-FACE, MVR transfers); sections/03_software.md (stream reaches, lakes and multi-aquifer wells traced as extra cells) --> The choice between a term and a cell changes how a trace splits a sink's water by source (example C in Section 4). <!-- evidence: sections/04_examples.md "Further examples" (tracing stream reaches as cells moves 32 % of the well's water from stream leakage to upstream inflow and recharge); outline.md §3.4 example C point 3 -->

If $\mathrm{Src}_i$ and $\mathrm{Snk}_i$ are the sums of the source and sink terms of cell $i$, its total inflow, total outflow and cell budget error are

$$I_i = \sum_j q_{ji} + \mathrm{Src}_i, \qquad O_i = \sum_j q_{ij} + \mathrm{Snk}_i, \qquad r_i = I_i - O_i. \tag{1}$$

The cell budgets of a MODFLOW model do not balance exactly, because the solver stops once the heads and residuals meet convergence tolerances. The cell budget error $r_i$ is usually small but not zero. <!-- evidence: facts review r2 finding 7 (wording; MODFLOW 6 IMS stops on a head-change criterion, DVCLOSE, and a residual criterion, RCLOSE) [check: cite the MODFLOW 6 IMS documentation or drop the "because" clause; no citable source for it is in the reference list yet] --> The code reports this error but never forces it to zero. Because F is written with each cell's outflow and G with its inflow, the error enters the water-budget checks that link them (below). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/diagnostics.py (cell_residual, budget_error; checks include the residual term); docs/paper/terms.md (cell budget error: reported, never forced to zero); structure review r2 finding 7 -->

**The backward fraction F.** The backward fraction answers the question of where the water of a sink comes from. We refer to the sink terms that a trace follows as the destination. The destination can be one well, one stream reach or a group of wells traced together. <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (destination: the sink term(s) traced to); src/gwsource/selectors.py (selection by package, boundary name, term, cells or zone); src/gwsource/transient.py GroupSpec (destination groups) --> The backward fraction $F_i$ is the share of the water leaving cell $i$ that later leaves the model through the destination. Let $d_i$ be the outflow of cell $i$ through the destination, and $P_{ij} = q_{ij}/O_i$ the share of its outflow that goes to cell $j$. Under full mixing, the water that cell $i$ sends to cell $j$ shares the fate of all the water leaving $j$. In volumes and in shares, this gives

$$F_i\, O_i = \sum_j q_{ij}\, F_j + d_i , \qquad F_i = \sum_j P_{ij}\, F_j + \frac{d_i}{O_i}. \tag{2}$$

A cell's F is the share of its outflow that leaves through the destination directly, plus the outflow-weighted sum of the F of its downstream neighbours. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) Eqs. (2) holds in every cell, so one solve of these linear equations gives the fractions to every destination group at once. <!-- evidence: si/B.md Eq. (S1) (each column of F holds one destination group); src/gwsource/solve.py (factorize, one factorisation for all right-hand sides) --> Where water circulates among cells, as it can with the XT3D option of MODFLOW 6 for anisotropic flow (Provost et al. 2017), the code solves each such flow loop exactly. FlowSource instead deletes the smallest flow in each loop (Foley and Black 2013; {SI: method in full}). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/config.py (solver "auto": topological substitution if acyclic, block-triangular sparse LU otherwise); src/gwsource/solve.py module docstring; si/B.md "Matrix form and solution of the linear systems" (references/docs/text/2013_Foley_Black_FlowSource_DAG_algorithms.txt, cycle section: "the edge with the lowest volume is removed"); structure review r2 finding 12 -->

A map of F shows where a destination's water comes from. A contributing area is the set of cells where F exceeds a threshold that the user chooses, such as 0.5. Because F takes in-between values at the edge of an area, the choice of threshold is a judgement (Section 6). The sum of F times the cell area gives a size of the area without a threshold. We refer to it as the expected area. <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (contributing area; expected area, sum of F); studies/paper/example_figures.py line 230 ("expected area (km2)": f @ area); structure review r2 minor 18 -->

**Gross and net accounting at weak sinks.** The well cell of Fig. 1a is a weak sink, because its well takes only part of the cell's outflow (Section 1). At a weak sink the method needs a rule for how much of the cell's water to attribute to the sink. Eq. (2) applies gross accounting (Section 1). Each sink term takes its share of the cell's total outflow. Only that share counts as the sink's water, while the rest of the cell's outflow passes on to its neighbours. <!-- evidence: Potter et al. 2008 p. 336, Fig. 1; docs/paper/terms.md (gross accounting; weak sink) --> Gross accounting is the default because it attributes to each sink only the water that the sink takes from the model, as the MODFLOW 6 Groundwater Transport (GWT) Model does (Langevin et al. 2022), so that the water traced to a well adds up to what it pumps. <!-- [author to confirm] --> <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/config.py (accounting "gross" default); docs/paper/terms.md (net attribution: under gross accounting it equals the pumping); studies/paper/gallery_figure.py make_method_single (gross destination inflow 6.0 = the well's 6 m³/d); PLAN.md line 282 (steady G equals GWT at equilibrium, the direct test of gross accounting) -->

Under net accounting, the destination cells are fixed at F = 1. All the water that flows into them from other cells then counts as the destination's water. This is how FlowSource attributes water at a well (Foley and Black 2013; {SI: method in full}). In Fig. 1a, net accounting adds the 5 m³/d that flow on through the well cell to the river. It leaves out the 1 m³/d of recharge on the well cell itself, because it works from the cell-to-cell flows only. The well is therefore attributed the 10 m³/d that flow into its cell from its neighbours, against the 6 m³/d it pumps. <!-- evidence: studies/paper/gallery_figure.py make_method_single (asserts the net destination inflow, 10.0 m³/d); studies/paper/method_figures.py GRAPH_EDGES, GRAPH_TERMS (cell 6: 9 + 1 in from neighbours, 1 recharge; 6 to the well, 5 on); facts review r2 must-fix 1 (net 10.0, gross 6.0, from gs.backward on graph_step); si/B.md Eq. **The forward fraction G.** The forward fraction answers the opposite question, which is where the water of a source goes. The forward fraction $G_i$ is the share of the water entering cell $i$ that came from the source. Let $s_i$ be the inflow of cell $i$ through the source. The volume of water entering cell $i$ that came from the source is then

$$G_i\, I_i = \sum_j q_{ji}\, G_j + s_i . \tag{3}$$

A cell's G is the share of its inflow that enters directly from the source, plus the inflow-weighted sum of the G of its upstream neighbours. Eq. (3) mirrors Eq. (2), with inflows in place of outflows. <!-- evidence: si/B.md (round 1) Eq. (S4); src/gwsource/assemble.py (forward rows G_i - sum_j (q_ji/I_i) G_j = sum_{k in S} q_k / I_i) --> A map of G shows how much of each cell's water came from the source (Fig. 2c). G equals the concentration that GWT reaches at equilibrium on the same grid, with upstream weighting and no dispersion, when the source water carries a concentration of 1 and all other water a concentration of 0 (Table 1, row 03). <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §9, cell 24 (GWT at equilibrium equals G in every cell; "for an upstream-weighted transport model"); PLAN.md line 282 (ADV UPSTREAM, no DSP, FLOW_IMBALANCE_CORRECTION); docs/paper/terms.md (transient tracking checks: steady G vs GWT at equilibrium); facts review r2 finding 5 -->

**Volumes and the water-budget checks.** A fraction says what share of a cell's water reaches the destination, but not how much water that is. A fraction times a flow gives a volume of water per unit time. We refer to these rates as volumes. The volume through cell $i$ is $F_i O_i$. It says how much of the water passing through the cell later reaches the destination. The volume from cell $i$ is $F_i$ times the cell's inflow through its source terms. It says how much water enters the model in that cell and later reaches the destination. The volumes from all cells, added up package by package, split the destination's water by source (Fig. 2b). In the same way, the volumes of a forward trace split a source's water by the sink through which it leaves. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/metrics.py docstring (volume_through = F x O, gross; volume_from, volume_from_by_term; forward volume_through = G x I, volume_to, volume_to_by_term) -->

Because they are volumes of water, the results of a backward or forward trace can be checked against the flows that MODFLOW 6 simulated. The closure check follows from adding Eq. (2) over all cells. It says that the source water traced to the destination, once corrected for the cell budget errors, adds up to the water the destination takes. The forward–backward check requires the volume from a source to a destination to be the same whether it is traced backward from the destination or forward from the source. The boundary-control check traces every sink of the model at once. It requires the fractions to all sinks to add up to 1 in every cell with outflow, so that the water of every cell leaves the model somewhere. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/diagnostics.py (backward_closure, forward_closure, duality, boundary control); docs/paper/terms.md (closure, forward–backward and boundary-control checks); si/B.md Eqs. (S3)–(S5) and the boundary-control paragraph; structure review r2 finding 9 --> All three checks hold exactly for any flow field, except in loops where water circulates without ever reaching a sink ({SI: method in full}). <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/diagnostics.py module docstring ("with the residual term the checks hold to round-off"); si/B.md boundary-control paragraph (a value of 1 solves the equation because sum_j q_ij + Snk_i = O_i; unique outside closed recirculation loops); facts review r2 finding 3 --> As a result, a check error larger than round-off points to a fault in the tracing. The checks test the solution of the equations. They cannot show that the flow model is right, or that full mixing describes how water moves in the aquifer. <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.5, last paragraph -->

**Transient flow: snapshots and transient tracking.** Everything so far assumes steady flow. In a transient model, the water that reaches a sink in one time step may have entered the aquifer in an earlier step. A snapshot traces each time step on its own flows, as if they were steady (Section 1). In a snapshot, water released from storage counts as a source term. Water taken into storage counts as a sink term (Fig. 1b, top). FlowSource (Foley and Black 2013) treats transient models in this way (Environment Agency 2019). <!-- evidence: draft/sections/02_methods.md Section 2.7, paragraph 1; draft_reduced/sections/01_introduction.md (Environment Agency 2019, Appendix C4.4); src/gwsource/transient.py (snapshots: storage release a source, storage gain a sink) --> A snapshot cannot show recharge of the wet season reaching a well in the dry season, because it assumes that water reaches the sink in the same step it entered the aquifer. We extend full mixing through time. We refer to this as transient tracking. Water from the chosen sources is tagged, as a tracer would be, and carried from step to step through the water volume of each cell (Fig. 1b, bottom). **Backward tracking in time.** Forward tracking says how much of each cell's water came from a source. It cannot say which of the water now in the aquifer will reach a sink, or by when. Backward tracking in time answers this question. It solves a balance of the same form as Eq. (5), with the roles of inflow and outflow swapped, from the last step of the simulation back to the first. <!-- evidence: si/B.md "Matrix form of transient tracking and backward tracking in time" (the adjoint holds the same flows with the roles of inflow and outflow swapped); src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring, backward (adjoint) scheme --> This differs from MODPATH backward tracking, in which particles start at the sink and move against the flow. Backward tracking in time is the discrete counterpart of the adjoint methods that solve a transport equation backward in time (Neupauer and Wilson 1999; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006). <!-- evidence: docs/paper/terms.md (backward tracking in time); src/gwsource/tracking.py module docstring, backward (adjoint) scheme; si/B.md "Backward tracking in time" -->

The backward fraction in time $w_i^n$ is the share of the water in cell $i$ at the start of step $n$, or entering it during the step, that leaves through the destination before the simulation ends. Over step $n$,

$$\Big[ V_i^{\mathrm{new}} + \Delta t^n\, (O_i + r_i) \Big]\, w_i^n = \Delta t^n \Big[ \sum_j q_{ij}\, w_j^n + d_i^n \Big] + V_i^{\mathrm{old},\,n+1}\, w_i^{n+1}. \tag{6}$$
