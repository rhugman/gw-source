# 5. A regional model: Neversink

This section shows that a trace works and stays fast on a published regional model. It also explains why full-mixing areas differ from the published particle areas, so that a practitioner can read one next to the other. The USGS Neversink/Rondout model (Fienen et al. 2022; Corson-Dosch et al. 2022) is a steady-state MODFLOW 6 model of 1.68 million cells. The published study refers to eight of its wells as priority wells. Together they pump 3,671 m³/d. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §1 (4 layers of 680 × 619 cells of 50 m, 1,683,680 cells; SFR 12,316 reaches; total pumping of the eight priority wells 3,671 m³/d); Corson-Dosch et al. 2022, title ("Areas contributing recharge to priority wells") --> The model has 358 realisations from history matching with PEST++ IES (Section 4). We refer to the realisation with the published calibrated parameter values as the calibrated model. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §6 and line 202 (rebuilt base realisation equals the published calibrated input); si/D.md (run 357 is the calibrated realisation); ensemble/summary.csv (358 realisations); round 2: White 2018 and White et al. 2020 cited once, in Section 4 (reviews/r2_examples_structure.md finding 14); "base parameters" replaced (r2_examples_prose.md 3.2 item 2; NS §1 "calibrated ("optimal", base-parameter)") -->

The published study mapped the wells' contributing area in the calibrated model with MODPATH 7 (Pollock 2016). One particle started in the uppermost active cell of each column near the wells. We refer to these cells as the start cells. The published contributing cells are the 980 start cells whose particle ends in a priority well. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §1, §2 (three regions, 122,021 particles); mp7_status.csv --> We traced the wells in the calibrated model with gross accounting. The stream reaches stayed source and sink terms of the aquifer cells. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §1, §2 (trace of the calibrated model with SFR leakage as a source and sink term of the aquifer cells, called "streams as cells" in NS; gross accounting; checks_cells.csv) -->

A trace of all the wells took a few seconds, less than the fastest PRT run on the same flows. On an Apple M4 Max with 64 GB of memory, one trace of nine destination groups (each well alone and all eight together) took 2–3 s and peaked at 2.5 GB of memory. Each further group added about 0.19 GB ({SI: the Neversink comparison}). <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md Summary "Cost" (2 to 3 s, nine destination groups); docs/paper/draft/reviews/r4_memory.md (load plus trace 2.4 to 2.7 s; peak RSS 2.52 GB for nine groups; (2.52 − 0.98) / 8 = 0.19 GB per further group; Apple M4 Max, 64 GB); round 2: hardware moved here from Section 3 (reviews/r2_examples_structure.md findings 6 and 13; r2_examples_facts.md findings 12 and 22) --> The closure check held to 1 × 10⁻¹² m³/d or better for every group. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §1 "Checks" (backward closure 1e-12 m³/d or smaller for every group, with streams as cells and as nodes) --> PRT took about 30 s when it wrote only where each particle ends. The full-mixing areas differ from the published area. The overlap of two areas is the number of cells in both divided by the number in either. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2 (Jaccard index) --> The published area comes from one reasonable choice of particle settings. The published runs put recharge and stream flows on the top face of a cell where no active cell lies above it. They stopped a particle in the first weak-sink cell it entered. They moved a particle that started in a dry cell down to the first active cell below. We refer to this setting as draping. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2 "End states" and §3 "Faces" (top face only where it is a model boundary: all recharge cells and 10,107 of the 12,316 reaches; the 2,209 buried reaches keep the flow spread through the cell; reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 7); §3 "Dry start cells" (MODPATH DrapeOption) --> PRT on the same flows reproduces the published area with these settings (overlap 0.998). Without the top-face setting, the overlap falls to 0.65. Without draping, it falls to 0.96. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §1, §2, §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; mp7_reproduction.csv (0.998 published settings; 0.96 without draping; 0.65 MF6 default faces, draped; 0.62 MF6 default, dropped); prt_comparison.csv; round 2: "only with all three settings" replaced, because stopping at weak sinks stays on in all four reproduction runs (reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 6) --> Two of our particle runs do not conserve water in the sense of Section 4. Particles that lose weight only at the wells deliver 2.6 times the wells' inflow (overlap 0.29). Particles released at the water table deliver 0.66 times it (overlap 0.39). Leaving these two runs out, the particle settings alone move the overlap from 0.62 to 0.998 ({SI: the Neversink comparison}). <!-- evidence: si/D.md table of delineations (losing weight only at wells: overlap 0.29, 9,555 m³/d; released at the water table: overlap 0.39, 2,413 m³/d; the remaining particle runs 0.62 to 0.998) and the note below it (9,555 m³/d, 2.6 times 3,671 m³/d); 2,413 / 3,671 = 0.66 (docs/neversink_study.md "Why the first PRT runs…" table, 66 %); reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 8; r2_examples_structure.md finding 9 -->

The full-mixing areas overlap the published area less than any of these particle runs do. The areas where F exceeds 0.5 and 0.1 have overlaps of 0.41 and 0.30. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2, Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv --> We infer that the main reason is the one seen in example A. Full mixing splits each cell's water among all its outflows, while a particle follows one path. On average, the published cells send 42 % of their water to the wells. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2 ("Their mean F is 0.42") --> Many of them therefore fall below F = 0.5 (Fig. 4a). The F > 0.1 area holds 73 % of them (711 of 980). Fig. 4b shows this for a window around four of the wells, where the F > 0.1 area holds 538 of the 688 published cells. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2 (F > 0.1 contains 711 of the 980 published cells); display/N.md and neversink_figures.py check_window (window counts 688; 538 in both, 150 published only); reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 25 -->

Weight-losing particles (Section 4) lose weight at each sink as full mixing does. For this reason they measure only the spreading that full mixing adds between sinks. The F > 0.1 area holds 87 % of the 691 start cells where these particles deliver more than half their water to the wells ({SI: Neversink particle area and ensemble mean F}a). <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5: F>0.1 coverage 0.871, 691 PRT cells); round 2: the caveat that the particles share full mixing at the sinks restored from si/D.md (reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 3), SI figure cited (finding 14) --> This share is an upper bound, because the particles started only in cells with F > 10⁻⁶. Weak sinks add to the difference. A buried stream cell holds a stream reach and has another active cell above it. Where such a reach gains water, it is a weak sink that stops published particles. In the published runs, 26,828 particles stopped in buried stream cells and 466 in the five priority wells that take only part of their cell's outflow. Each stopped particle counts in full as reaching that sink, while full mixing passes the rest of the cell's water on ({SI: the Neversink comparison}). <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §2 "End states" (status 3: 26,828 stopped in stream cells with an active cell above; 466 in the five weak-sink priority wells) --> Net accounting attributes to a weak-sink well all the water that enters its cell. It raises the F > 0.5 overlap only from 0.41 to 0.44. At the weak-sink wells, gross accounting comes closer to the published cell counts than net accounting does. <!-- evidence: si/D.md, table of delineations; docs/neversink_study.md §2, Tables 2a–2b; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv; round 2: "much as a stopping particle does" removed, because NS §2 below Table 2b rejects that reading ("The per-well counts do not support this, since gross accounting is closer at the weak-sink wells"; Table 2c, U___7 94 to 214 cells against 58 published; reviews/r2_examples_facts.md finding 4) -->

Example C (Section 4) showed that a sink's split by source depends on how stream reaches are traced. On this model the split also depends on the method. With the stream reaches left as source and sink terms, full mixing attributes 57 % of the wells' water to recharge and 43 % to stream leakage. With the reaches traced as cells of their own, all of the wells' water traces back to recharge. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md Summary "Streams" and §4 (all 3,671 m³/d traces to recharge with the SFR reaches as cells of the traced system) --> Along weight-losing particle paths, with the reaches again left as source and sink terms, the split is 49 % recharge and 51 % stream leakage. Both methods deliver the wells' total inflow to within 2 %. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §3 (prt_sources_by_well.csv, one PRT run evaluated for every well: 57/43 under full mixing, 49/51 along the particle paths); §4 (2,100 m³/d, 57 %, recharge; 1,571 m³/d, 43 %, SFR leakage); §3, Table 3 (weight-losing particles over the cell volume 3,736 m³/d, 1.8 % above the wells' inflow of 3,671 m³/d) --> We infer, without tracing it cell by cell, that stream water leaking near the wells stays on particle paths that end at a well. Full mixing instead gives that water the average fate of all the water leaving the cells it passes. <!-- evidence: docs/neversink_study.md §7 "Why thinned particles grow faster" (inferred, not traced cell by cell) and §8 --> A split by source on a regional model should therefore be reported with the method that gave it.

For the practitioner, a particle area depends on its particle settings as much as on the flow model. The comparison that isolates full mixing is a particle run that loses weight at sinks as full mixing does. Against that run, the F > 0.1 area held 87 % of the particle area on this model, so a low threshold on F did not give a complete outer limit. <!-- round 2: closing reading guide (reviews/r2_examples_structure.md finding 5); the advice itself is for Section 6 -->
