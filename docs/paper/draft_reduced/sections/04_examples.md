# 4. Worked examples

Four of the ten worked examples follow. The last part of this section summarises the other six. Fig. 2 shows one output of each type. All examples start from one small example model. It is a single unconfined layer of 40 × 40 cells of 50 m. Recharge of 5 × 10⁻⁴ m/d falls on every cell. A constant head along the west edge brings in regional groundwater. A river runs north–south 100 m east of a supply well that pumps 800 m³/d. <!-- evidence: tutorials/herebedragons.py AquiferSpec (nrow = ncol = 40, delr = delc = 50 m, top 20 m, botm −30 m, k 5 m/d, rech 5e-4 m/d, chd_head 10 m, riv_col 30 (0-based), riv_stage 6 m, supply_well (20, 28) −800 m³/d) and build_quickstart (icelltype 1); tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §1, cell 4 output (row 21, column 29; river in column 31) --> Every example model is described in {SI: example and test models}.

**Sources and area.** A protection zone starts from the land whose water reaches the well. Fig. 2a maps F. Cells with F > 0.5 cover 1.143 km². Cells with F > 0.1 cover 1.575 km². <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §5, cell 16 (F > 0.1: 630 cells, 1.575 km²; F > 0.5: 457 cells, 1.143 km²) --> The same trace splits the 800 m³/d pumped into 71 % recharge, 15 % inflow at the west edge and 14 % river leakage (Fig. 2b). <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §6, cell 20 (recharge 568.6 m³/d, 0.711; west edge 118.1, 0.148; river 113.3, 0.142) --> The traced sources add up to the pumping, as the closure check requires. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §7, cell 22 (800.0000 against 800.0000 m³/d, relative closure error 0.0) --> That check tests the solution but not the position of the area's edge.

The code can run MODFLOW 6 PRT on the same flows to test the edge. Particles start from every source term in cells with F > 0. Each carries a share of its term's flow as a weight. A particle loses weight at every other sink it passes, in proportion to the flow that sink takes. Visser et al. (2009) split particles at weak sinks in a similar way to derive travel times. Winston et al. (2018) reduce particle weights at sinks in a solute-transport method. We refer to these as weight-losing particles. They deliver 799.9 of the 800 m³/d pumped. That total says little about the area, because particles that conserve water must deliver what the well pumps. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/prt.py track_particles (seed from the backward result's cells with F > threshold, write and run PRT, evaluate the destination share; option thin_weak_sinks=True gives the weight loss at weak sinks); tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (18,208 particles from 1,133 cells; 799.9 of 800.0 m³/d) --> The particle share of a cell is the share of the weight released there that reaches the well. Cells with a particle share above 0.5 cover 1.168 km². The F > 0.5 area is 2.1 % smaller. The F > 0.1 area is 35 % larger. It holds every cell of the particle area. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (P > 0.5: 467 cells, 1.168 km²; F > 0.5 −2.1 %, 14 particle cells outside; F > 0.1 +34.9 %, 0 particle cells outside) --> In the 372 cells with F between 0.05 and 0.95, the particle share and F differ by 0.164 on average ({SI: contributing area against particle tracking}). <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (mean |P − F| 0.164 over 372 edge cells) --> In those cells, most particles either reach the well or miss it. Full mixing gives the cell an in-between value.

The choice between gross and net accounting (Section 2) matters only at weak sinks. In a three-layer version of the model, a small well pumps 5 m³/d from a cell that carries 33.7 m³/d. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 13 (inflow 33.67 m³/d, taken by the well 5.00, passed on 28.67) --> Gross accounting attributes the 5 m³/d it pumps to the well. Net accounting attributes all 33.7 m³/d. **Transient tracking.** A manager may ask how much of the water pumped over the coming decades is already in the aquifer today. This example makes the model transient, with seasonal recharge and pumping. It runs for 30 years, with one time step per season. The run starts from a steady state in which the well already pumps. Initial water is therefore not water from before pumping began. <!-- evidence: tutorials/herebedragons.py build_transient (sy 0.2, ss 1e-5), SEASON_RECHARGE (1.8, 1.0, 0.1, 1.1), SEASON_PUMPING (0.8, 1.0, 1.4, 0.8); tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb Settings and §1 (N_YEARS 30, STEPS_PER_SEASON 1, POROSITY 0.3; 121 time steps; specific yield 0.2) --> <!-- Left out for length, in {SI: transient checks on the seasonal example model} and {SI: method in full}: each flow step is split into four transport sub-steps, which shorten the transport step without changing the flow model (tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb Settings, TRANSPORT_SUBSTEPS 4, and §9) -->

Averaged over time, snapshots give the well's water as about 60 % recharge and 15 % storage release ({SI: snapshots and transient tracking over 30 years}a). <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3, cell 8 (snapshots, time-weighted mean: recharge 0.60, river 0.14, west edge 0.12, storage 0.15) --> With transient tracking, the well's water in the last season of year 30 is 51.6 % initial water, 32.9 % recharge and 15.5 % river water. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3 table (year 30: initial 0.516, recharge 0.329, river 0.155, west edge 0.0) --> Of all the water pumped in the first 10 years, 81 % was initial water. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §4 table (initial water, all pumped so far: 0.976, 0.813 and 0.677 after 1, 10 and 30 years) --> New recharge mixes into a large volume of older water, because a column of this aquifer holds about 64 years of recharge. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3 (water column 11.4 m, recharge 0.18 m/yr, 64 years) --> With one layer, the model cannot hold young water above old water. Backward tracking in time shows that only the water near the well and along the main flow path reaches the well within 30 years (Fig. 2f). **Ensembles.** Many sets of parameters can fit the measured heads about equally well. We refer to each set as a realisation. A set of realisations is an ensemble. Here 50 realisations of the example model each have a random hydraulic conductivity field. No measurements have shaped these fields. We refer to such an ensemble as a prior. <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb Settings, cell 2 (N_REAL 50, PRIOR_SEED 3, SIGMA_LNK 0.8, CORRELATION_LENGTH 400 m, PP_SPACING 4) and §1, cells 3 and 4 (K = 5 exp(0.8 z) at 100 pilot points, simple kriging of ln K; "These realisations are a prior: no data has shaped them") --> The probability of contribution of a cell is the share of realisations in which F > 0.5.

History matching adjusts a model's parameters until it fits measurements. PEST++ IES does this for every realisation of an ensemble (White 2018; White et al. 2020). A separate random field stands for the true aquifer. Its heads in 40 cells, with added noise, are the measurements ({SI: ensemble and history-matching set-up}). <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §9 intro and §9.1, cells 21 and 22 (truth from hbd.build_realisation, not from pilot points; 40 head observations, noise sd 0.05 m) --> The history-matched ensemble is the posterior. History matching narrows the probability of contribution (Fig. 2g; {SI: probability of contribution before and after history matching}). The probability lies between 0.1 and 0.9 over 1.10 km² in the prior and 0.35 km² in the posterior. The middle 90 % of river shares narrows from 0–33 % to 5–13 %. The true share is 9.4 %. <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §8, cell 20 (0.80 km² for 0.1 < F < 0.9 in the homogeneous base case); §9.4, cell 28 (prior, posterior: band 0.1 < P < 0.9 1.098, 0.350 km²; river share 5 % and 95 % 0.000–0.330, 0.047–0.134; truth river share 0.094) and cell 29 -->

A narrower posterior is not necessarily a correct one. The posterior gives a probability of at least 0.5 to 93 % of the true F > 0.5 area, against 79 % for the prior. <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §9.4, cell 28 (true zone with P ≥ 0.5: 79.268 and 93.415 %) --> In both ensembles, 86 to 87 % of the cells with a probability of at least 0.9 lie inside the true area. That share did not improve. <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §9.4, cell 28 (area P ≥ 0.9 inside the true zone: 86.099 and 87.468 %) and cell 29 --> One synthetic case cannot show how reliable posteriors are in general. Here the trace ran on each realisation after PEST++ had finished. We have not tested running it inside each PEST++ model run. <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §9 (IES run on the prior of §1, then gs.run_ensemble on the posterior); forward-run use untested (full draft Section 4.9) -->
