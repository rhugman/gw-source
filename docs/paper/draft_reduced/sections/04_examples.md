# 4. Worked examples

This section shows how a practitioner can use the code to answer three common questions, and how far each answer can be trusted. Each example checks its result against another common approach, such as particle tracking, transport with tagged water or a published study. Example A maps where a well's water comes from. Example B follows that water through a transient model. Example C splits the well's water by source and by travel time. Table 1, at the end of this section, summarises all ten examples that ship with the code. Fig. 2 shows one output of each type. <!-- structure: outline §3.4 opening paragraph; decisions 2, 3 and 6 (three examples in depth, the rest in Table 1, ensembles a table row) -->

All examples start from one small example model. It is a single unconfined layer of 40 × 40 cells of 50 m. Recharge of 5 × 10⁻⁴ m/d falls on every cell. A constant head along the west edge brings in regional groundwater. **Example A: where does the well's water come from?** A protection zone around a supply well starts from the land whose water reaches the well. For this reason, a practitioner needs to know how large that area is, how far it reaches and how much to trust its edge. Fig. 2a maps F for the supply well. Cells with F > 0.5 cover 1.143 km². Cells with F > 0.1 cover 1.575 km². The expected area of Section 2 gives a size without a threshold. Here it lies between the two, at 1.173 km². <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §5, cell 16 (F > 0.1: 630 cells, 1.575 km²; F > 0.5: 457 cells, 1.143 km²; sum of F × cell area 1.173 km²) --> The F > 0.1 area reaches the west edge of the model, about 1.4 km from the well. It reaches this far because 15 % of the well's water enters at the west edge as regional inflow. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §6, cell 20 (west edge 118.1 m³/d, share 0.148); tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §4, cell 17 output (DIS: well cell centre at x = 1,425 m; constant-head cells with centres at x = 25 m); the F > 0.1 contour reaches the west edge in Fig. 2a (read off the figure) --> The area also extends east of the river. Near the well the river loses water to the aquifer, so groundwater from the east can pass through the river cells to the well. <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §4, cell 12 output ("losing river reaches in rows 19 to 23"); Fig. 2a (F > 0.1 and F > 0.5 contours east of the river, read off the figure) -->

The closure check tests the trace behind these numbers. The traced sources add up to the 800 m³/d pumped, with a relative closure error of 0.0, as the closure check requires. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §7, cell 22 (800.0000 against 800.0000 m³/d, relative closure error 0.0) --> This check tests the solution of the equations, but it cannot test the position of the area's edge. The edge is where full mixing and particle tracking are expected to differ, so it needs a check of its own. For this check the code runs MODFLOW 6 PRT on the same flows. Particles start from every source term in cells with F > 0. Each particle carries a share of its term's flow as a weight. A particle loses weight at every other sink it passes, in proportion to the flow that sink takes from the cell. This is the particle counterpart of gross accounting. Visser et al. (2009) split particles at weak sinks in a similar way to derive travel times. Winston et al. (2018) reduce particle weights at sinks in a solute-transport method. We refer to these as weight-losing particles. <!-- evidence: src/gwsource/prt.py track_particles (seed from the backward result's cells with F > threshold, write and run PRT, evaluate the destination share; option thin_weak_sinks=True gives the weight loss at weak sinks) -->

The 18,208 particles from 1,133 cells deliver 799.9 of the 800 m³/d pumped. That total says little about the area, because particles that conserve water must deliver what the well pumps. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (18,208 particles from 1,133 cells; 799.9 of 800.0 m³/d) --> The comparison of areas is more telling. The particle share of a cell is the share of the weight released there that reaches the well (Fig. 3a). Cells with a particle share above 0.5 cover 1.168 km². The F > 0.5 area is 2.1 % smaller and leaves out 14 of the particle cells. The F > 0.1 area is 35 % larger. It holds every cell of the particle area. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (P > 0.5: 467 cells, 1.168 km²; F > 0.5 −2.1 %, 14 particle cells outside; F > 0.1 +34.9 %, 0 particle cells outside) --> The two methods differ most at the edge. In the 372 cells with F between 0.05 and 0.95, the particle share and F differ by 0.164 on average. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (mean |P − F| 0.164 over the 372 edge cells, 0.058 over all 1,133 release cells) --> In those cells, most particles either reach the well or miss it. Full mixing instead gives each cell an in-between value, because it splits the cell's water among all its outflows.

**Example B: how much of the pumped water is already in the aquifer?** A manager may ask how much of the water pumped over the coming decades is already in the aquifer today. The answer matters where new recharge differs in quality from the water already stored, for example after a change in land use. Snapshots cannot answer this question (Section 2). A seasonal version of the example model shows why. In this version, recharge is multiplied by 1.8, 1.0, 0.1 and 1.1 in the wet, wet-to-dry, dry and dry-to-wet seasons. The well pumps between 640 and 1,120 m³/d. <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §1, cell 4 output (recharge factors 1.8, 1.0, 0.1, 1.1; pumping 640, 800, 1,120, 640 m³/d); tutorials/herebedragons.py SEASON_RECHARGE, SEASON_PUMPING --> In snapshots of a 3-year run, with three time steps per season, the F > 0.5 area grows from 0.59 km² in the wet season to 1.35 km² in the dry season. <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §3, cell 6 (F > 0.5 area: wet 0.592, dry 1.350 km²); si/C.md "Seasonal model" (the snapshot example runs 3 years with three time steps per season) --> In the dry season, the snapshots attribute 52.5 % of the well's water to storage. <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §4, cell 9 (mean share by season: dry storage 0.525) --> A snapshot cannot say where that stored water came from. It may be old water or recharge of the last wet season. Statistics over 36 snapshots can still combine the seasonal maps into nested zones (Fig. 2e; Table 1, row 05). <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §5, cell 12 (36 steps) and §7, cell 21 (inner 0.52, seasonal 0.93, ring 0.41 km²) -->

Transient tracking follows the stored water instead. For transient tracking, the seasonal model runs for 30 years, with one time step per season and 121 time steps in all. The tracking uses a porosity of 0.3 and four transport sub-steps per flow step. The run starts from a steady state in which the well already pumps. Initial water is therefore not water from before pumping began. <!-- evidence: tutorials/herebedragons.py build_transient (sy 0.2, ss 1e-5); tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb Settings and §1 (N_YEARS 30, STEPS_PER_SEASON 1, POROSITY 0.3, TRANSPORT_SUBSTEPS 4; cell 4 output, 121 time steps); si/C.md "Seasonal model" (the well already pumps at its mean rate in the steady-state period) --> Averaged over time, snapshots of the 30-year run give the well's water as about 60 % recharge and 15 % storage release ({SI: snapshots and transient tracking over 30 years}a). <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3, cell 8 (snapshots, time-weighted mean: recharge 0.60, river 0.14, west edge 0.12, storage 0.15) --> Transient tracking gives a different answer. In the last season of year 30, the well's water is 51.6 % initial water, 32.9 % recharge and 15.5 % river water. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3, cell 8 table (year 30: initial 0.516, recharge 0.329, river 0.155, west edge 0.0) --> Of all the water pumped in the first 10 years, 81 % was initial water. This share is 98 % for the first year and 68 % over 30 years. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §4, cell 12 table (initial water, all pumped so far: 0.976, 0.813 and 0.677 after 1, 10 and 30 years) --> New recharge mixes into a large volume of older water, because a column of this aquifer holds about 64 years of recharge. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3, cell 10 (water column 11.4 m, recharge 0.18 m/yr, 64 years) --> With one layer, the model cannot hold young water above old water. A layered model may therefore give other shares.

Backward tracking in time shows which of the water now in the aquifer reaches the well. Only the water near the well and along the main flow path reaches it within 30 years (Fig. 2f). Most of the water in the steady-state contributing area is still on its way at the end. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §5, cells 14 and 15 ("Within 30 years, only the water near the well and along the main flow paths reaches it. Most of the capture zone's water is still on its way at the end", read off the maps) --> Of the 8.77 million m³ pumped over 30 years, backward tracking attributes 67.7 % to initial water. This matches the 68 % of the forward run. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §5, cell 16 (RIV 0.138, RCH 0.185, initial water 0.677; 8,766,800 m³ pumped) and §4, cell 12 (0.677 after 30 years) -->

Transient tracking was checked against GWT on the same model. It shows that the code solves Eq. (5) as GWT does. The choice of water volumes changes the results little on this model. Here porosity (0.3) differs from specific yield (0.2), so the two volume options of Section 2 give different water volumes. The share of initial water in year 30 is 0.5166 with the default volumes and 0.5167 with GWT's volumes. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §7, cell 20 ("largest difference between the modes: 1.9e-04 in c, 3.7e-03 in w (any cell, any step)"; well water that is initial, year 30: 0.5166 flow, 0.5167 gwt); herebedragons.py build_transient (sy 0.2) --> Transport sub-steps matter more. Here, four sub-steps remove most of this effect.

Backward tracking in time also gives the time-limited contributing area ({SI: time-limited contributing areas in the unit-concentration example}). The published study does not report it. **Example C: how much water comes from each source, and how old is it?** Many studies need the water of a sink split by source more than the edge of its area. How much of a well's water is river water? How old is the water from each source? These answers depend much less on mixing than the edge of an area does, so a practitioner can use them with more confidence. The backward trace of example A splits the 800 m³/d pumped into 71 % recharge, 15 % inflow at the west edge and 14 % river leakage (Fig. 2b). <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §6, cell 20 (recharge 568.6 m³/d, 0.711; west edge 118.1, 0.148; river 113.3, 0.142) --> A forward trace from each source gives the same split. The forward and backward volumes from each source to the well agree within 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³/d, as the forward–backward check requires. <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §3, cell 10 (G in the well cell: river 0.142, west edge 0.148, recharge 0.711) and §7, cell 18 (differences 2.8 × 10⁻⁹ to 4.7 × 10⁻⁷ m³/d, each equal to the cell budget error term) --> In the test problems of {SI: numerical dispersion test problems}, the shares by source were the same to three digits under full mixing, face-to-face routing and particles. <!-- evidence: docs/dispersion_study.md Summary, third bullet ("The shares of the well water by source package are identical to three digits under mixing, routing and particles on the same DIS grid") -->

The split by source also depends on where the trace stops. A second version of the example model shows this. In it, the river becomes a stream that flows from north to south. Two drains pass their water to the stream through the water mover. <!-- evidence: si/C.md "Streams, drains and linked models" (SFR, one reach per row, north to south; first reach receives 300 m³/d; two drains in column 15 pass 80 % and 100 % of their water to the first reach through MVR); tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §1 --> Its well therefore takes a different mix of water from the well above, so the shares below cannot be compared with the 14 % of river leakage above. When the stream reaches stay source and sink terms of the aquifer cells, stream leakage supplies 32 % of the well's water. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §2, cell 8 (SFR 255.2 m³/d, share 0.319) --> When the reaches are traced as cells of their own, that water splits into 129 m³/d of inflow at the upstream end of the stream and 126 m³/d of recharge that reached the stream through the drains. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §3, cell 20 (SFR:EXT-INFLOW 129.4; RCH 670.6 against 544.8, +125.8 m³/d) --> The closure check holds both ways. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §3, cell 20 (relative error 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ cells only, 0.0 with SFR nodes) --> The answer to "how much of the well's water is stream water?" thus depends on whether the stream reaches are traced. For this reason, a study should report which choice it made.

With a porosity of 0.3, the backward trace also gives mean travel times (Fig. 2d). The times are taken to the point where the water enters the well cell, because particle trackers stop particles there ({SI: method in full}). The mean travel time is 2.1 years for river leakage, 41.6 years for recharge and 108.4 years for inflow at the west edge. Over all the water, it is 45.9 years. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §3, cell 16 output (to the well cell: river 2.1, recharge 41.6, west edge 108.4, all sources 45.9 yr; porosity 0.3 from Settings) --> Weight-losing particles give 45.7 years for all the water, 0.5 % less. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6, cell 20 ("Overall means": PRT 45.7, full mixing 45.9 yr, −0.5 %) --> This agreement is not an independent test. Any method that conserves water gives the mean travel time of all the water as the volume of water bound for the sink divided by its inflow. The agreement therefore tests the volume attributed to the sink. That volume is nearly the same in both methods. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §5, cell 14 (volume identity, relative error 0.0; volume / pumping rate 16,791.3 d) and the text after "Overall means"; outline §3.4 [From Methods] point --> By source, the particle times differ by −2.1 % for recharge, +2.1 % for the west edge and −20.1 % for river leakage. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6, cell 20 (recharge −2.1 %, west edge +2.1 %, river leakage −20.1 %) -->

The comparison cell by cell is the informative one. In the 456 cells with F ≥ 0.5, apart from the well cell, the median ratio of particle time to full-mixing time is 0.96. In the 676 cells with F < 0.5, it is 5.72. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6, cell 22 (F ≥ 0.5: 456 cells, median 0.96; F < 0.5: 676 cells, median 5.72; the comparison keeps cells with a full-mixing time above 0, so the well cell drops out of the 457 cells with F ≥ 0.5) --> A particle from a cell with small F follows one slow path around the edge of the area. Full mixing averages over all the paths that the cell's water takes. The same difference shows in time-of-travel zones. The 25-year zone covers 0.455 km² under full mixing and 0.465 km² for particles, 2.2 % more. The 5-year zone covers 0.098 against 0.122 km², 25.6 % more. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §7, cell 24 (5 yr: 0.098 and 0.122 km², +0.256; 25 yr: 0.455 and 0.465 km², +0.022) --> On this evidence, we would take a well's water by source and its overall mean travel time from full mixing. We would draw a short time-of-travel zone with particles (Section 6).
