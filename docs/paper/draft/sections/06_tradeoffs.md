# 6. Trade-offs and limitations

Full mixing answers most questions of Section 4 in seconds. This section sets out what that costs, which choices it leaves to the modeller and when particles or transport answer a question better.

Full mixing splits each cell's water among all its outflows. A particle instead follows one path through the velocity field of each cell (Pollock 1988). So full-mixing areas are wider and more diffuse than particle areas. Their edge is a band of cells where F lies between 0.1 and 0.9. Finer cells narrow this band, but slowly. Because mixing blurs the edge, no single threshold on F reproduces a particle area everywhere. The threshold is a judgement about the purpose of the area. It should be reported with the area. On the simple test problems of Section 4.1, the F > 0.5 area came close to the size of the reference area. The F > 0.1 area contained the reference area, at the cost of more area. <!-- evidence: Section 4.1 (dispersion tests: F ≥ 0.5 within 5 % at h ≤ 50 m; F ≥ 0.1 missed ≤ 0.1 % and was 8 to 90 % larger at h = 12.5 to 50 m); tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (0 particle cells outside F > 0.1) --> On Neversink, the F > 0.1 area missed 27 % of the published contributing cells (Section 5). Weak sinks raise a second choice, between gross and net accounting (Section 2.4). Gross accounting answers where the pumped water comes from. Its volumes match those of a transport model. Net accounting answers which water reaches the well's cell, much as particles that stop at weak sinks do. At a small well the two can differ several times over (Section 4.2). We would use gross accounting by default. Net accounting is the choice for reproducing a FlowSource study. The accounting should be reported with the area, because it changes the area upstream of every weak-sink well.

Transient models need a further choice, among snapshots, transient tracking and GWT. Snapshots trace each time step on its own flows in seconds without a porosity (Section 4.5). But a snapshot has no travel lag. Water released from storage also appears in it as a source with no origin. Transient tracking carries tagged water through storage, as hydraulic mixing cells do in integrated surface water–groundwater models (Partington et al. 2011, 2013). <!-- references/docs/text/2011_Partington_etal_hydraulic_mixing_cell.txt (volumetric balance of each flow component over each time step, from the previous step's storage); previous/01_introduction.md:15 --> Forward, it gives the share of pumped water that was already stored. Backward in time, it gives the area whose water reaches the well within a stated time (Sections 4.8 and 4.10). It needs a porosity, the budget of every time step and, where arrival times matter, transport sub-steps (Section 4.8). With GWT's water volumes, transient tracking matched MODFLOW 6 GWT within GWT's solver tolerance (Sections 4.8 and 4.10). GWT needs one transport model per source. <!-- evidence: sections/04b_examples.md §4.8 (9.8 × 10⁻¹⁴, round-off level, recharge tag, one transport step per flow step); sections/04d_examples.md §4.10 (1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰, below the GWT solver tolerance of 10⁻⁹, four GWT runs); both with volume_mode "gwt" (review alt_r1 F3) --> We would use GWT where dispersion, sorption or decay matter (Langevin et al. 2022). Transient tracking moves water by advection only. It has so far been tested on synthetic models only.
