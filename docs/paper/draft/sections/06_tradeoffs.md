# 6. Trade-offs and limitations

Full mixing answers most questions of Section 4 in seconds. This section sets out what that costs, which choices it leaves to the modeller and when particles or transport answer a question better.

Full mixing splits each cell's water among all its outflows. A particle instead follows one path through the velocity field of each cell (Pollock 1988). So full-mixing areas are wider and more diffuse than particle areas. Their edge is a band of cells where F lies between 0.1 and 0.9. In tests against analytical and particle references, this band narrowed only as about the square root of the cell size (Fig. 13). <!-- evidence: docs/dispersion_study.md Summary and §2 (band 1.2 to 12 cells wide; width narrows as about h^0.5, fitted rates 0.4 to 0.6) --> Potter et al. (2008) and McLane and Cecan (2009) also found wider spreading on coarser grids. <!-- Potter p. 338: with zero dispersivity the spread is numerical dispersion, and the areas of uncertain capture shrink on finer grids; McLane and Cecan p. 172 and Fig. 1: spread grows with grid spacing, not hydrodynamic dispersion (wording as in previous/01_introduction.md:11) --> Thick layers spread an area vertically as well, because each cell mixes over its saturated thickness. Face-to-face routing passes water between cell faces instead of mixing it (Chowdhury 2016). In the tests, this experimental 2D option removed 35–70 % of the excess spreading. <!-- evidence: docs/dispersion_study.md Summary, §4 and §6 (35 to 70 %; cells with weak sinks or vertical through-flow stay fully mixed; the prototype is 2D) --> Totals depend much less on mixing. On cells of 50 m or less, the well's shares by source and the mean travel time of all its water stayed within 2 % of particle tracking. <!-- evidence: docs/dispersion_study.md §2 (shares identical to three digits under full mixing, face routing and PRT) and §5 (integral outputs within 2 % for h ≤ 50 m); Section 4.6 (−0.5 % against weight-losing PRT) --> We would therefore use particles where a sharp edge or a first arrival matters, such as a short time-of-travel zone (Section 4.6). Full mixing suits questions about many sinks, unstructured grids, package networks or many realisations. <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, pp. 76–78 (MODPATH time-of-travel zones for SPZ1 and SPZ2; total catchments checked against the full-mixing outputs) -->

Weak sinks raise a second choice, between gross and net accounting (Section 2.4). Gross accounting credits a well with the water it pumps. It answers where the pumped water comes from. Its volumes match those of a transport model. Net accounting credits a well with all the water that passes through its cell. It answers which water reaches the well's cell, much as particles that stop at weak sinks do. At a small well the two can differ several times over (Section 4.2). We would use gross accounting by default. Net accounting is the choice for reproducing a FlowSource study. The accounting should be reported with the area, because it changes the area upstream of every weak-sink well.

Transient models need a further choice, among snapshots, transient tracking and GWT. Snapshots trace each time step on its own flows in seconds without a porosity (Section 4.5). But a snapshot has no travel lag. Water released from storage also appears in it as a source with no origin. Transient tracking carries tagged water through storage, as hydraulic mixing cells do in integrated surface water–groundwater models (Partington et al. 2011, 2013). <!-- references/docs/text/2011_Partington_etal_hydraulic_mixing_cell.txt (volumetric balance of each flow component over each time step, from the previous step's storage); previous/01_introduction.md:15 --> Forward, it gives the share of pumped water that was already stored. Backward in time, it gives the area whose water reaches the well within a stated time (Sections 4.8 and 4.10). It needs a porosity, the budget of every time step and, where arrival times matter, transport sub-steps (Section 4.8). MODFLOW 6 GWT matched transient tracking within its solver tolerance, with one transport model per source (Sections 4.8 and 4.10). <!-- evidence: sections/04b_examples.md §4.8 (9.8 × 10⁻¹⁴); sections/04d_examples.md §4.10 (1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰, below the GWT solver tolerance of 10⁻⁹, four GWT runs) --> We would use GWT where dispersion, sorption or decay matter (Langevin et al. 2022). Transient tracking moves water by advection only. Transient tracking has so far been tested on synthetic models only.

The choices above also differ in cost. A steady-state trace is cheap. On the Neversink model it took 2–3 s and peaked at 2.5 GB, plus about 0.19 GB for each further destination group (Section 3.4). Time series and ensemble percentiles cost more. Transient tracking keeps by default one value per cell and time step for each group and output. At 1.68 million cells and 121 steps, we estimate about 1.6 GB per array. <!-- src/gwsource/transient.py TransientConfig.keep_series (default True); 1,683,680 × 121 × 8 bytes = 1.63 × 10⁹ bytes; an estimate, not a run (previous/07_limitations.md) --> The code can instead keep only the last step, at the price of the time series. Exact percentile maps of an ensemble keep every realisation's values. For 1,000 realisations of 1 million cells, they need 4 GB for each output of each destination. Histograms fix this at about 0.8 GB, with an error of one bin width (Section 4.9). <!-- evidence: tutorials/09_ensembles/09_ensembles.ipynb §6, cell 16 (six series: exact stack 24 GB at 1,000 realisations; histograms 4.8 GB with 200 bins, so 0.8 GB per series) -->

Some parts of a MODFLOW 6 simulation are not read yet. Movers between models (the MVE package) are not read. Water moved between models therefore appears as a sink in one model and a source in the other (Section 4.7). A DISU grid without cell vertices gives fractions but no maps (Section 4.4). PRT runs on DIS and DISV grids only.
