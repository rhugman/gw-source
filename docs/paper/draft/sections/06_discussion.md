## 6.1 Full mixing or particle tracking

Full mixing and weight-losing particles delivered the same volume of water to the Neversink wells, within 2 % (Section 5.3). <!-- NS §3, Table 3: 3,736 vs 3,671 m³/d, +1.8 %; prt_comparison.csv --> These particles treat sinks as full mixing does (Section 3.2), so the two methods differ only in how water spreads between sinks. Full mixing spreads it over more cells, each with a lower F. The F > 0.1 area held 87 % of the particles' P > 0.5 area. The F > 0.5 area held 59 % of it (Section 5.3). <!-- NS §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5: F>0.1 coverage 0.871; F>0.5 coverage 0.590) -->

Each method gives something the other cannot. Full mixing traces many destinations at once, in 2–3 s on Neversink (Section 5.1). <!-- NS Summary (Cost) --> Each trace also gives the share of water every weak sink takes, checked against the water budget. Particles give flow paths and first arrival times. Their paths also keep a sharp edge. Mixing blurs the edge of an area into a transition band, where F lies between 0.1 and 0.9. In the synthetic tests this band was 1.2 to 12 cells wide (Section 4.3). <!-- DS Summary ("the band between F = 0.1 and F = 0.9 is 1.2 to 12 cells wide") --> In a small zone the band can cover much of the zone. For such zones we would combine full mixing with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019, Appendix C4.3–C4.4) does for inner zones. <!-- EA 2019 manual, references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, pp. 76–78: MODPATH time-of-travel zones are the basis of SPZ1 (50-day) and SPZ2, total capture zones are checked against the full-mixing outputs, and the full-mixing code's own 50- and 400-day travel times come from one particle per cell -->

## 6.2 Gross or net accounting

Gross accounting credits a weak-sink well with what it pumps (Section 2.3). Net accounting, as in FlowSource, credits it with all the water that passes through its cell. On Neversink, net accounting raised the expected area from 910 to 978 cells, close to the 980 published contributing cells. <!-- NS §2, Table 2b; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (priority 910.2, 978.5; jaccard_F>0.5 0.41, 0.44) --> Well by well, the match fails. Net accounting exceeds the published count at three wells but falls below it at the other five ({SI: per-well gross and net}). <!-- NS §2, Tables 2a–2b; evidence: si/F_editor.md {SI: per-well gross and net} table (net against published: SV_407 37/32, U___7 214/58, U___8 249/163 above; SV_193 26/48, SV_227 74/115, SV_487 94/97, U_917 314/351, U__55 97/116 below); net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (sum of net expected_cells 1,103.6); net_vs_gross_wells.csv (dest_to_dest_flow 607.7, dest_onward_to_dest 70.1) --> The close total for the eight wells together comes partly from errors of opposite sign cancelling out. It gives no reason to prefer net accounting.

## 6.3 Snapshots, transient tracking or a GWT run

Snapshots trace each time step on its own flows, in seconds and without a porosity (Section 2.7). They cannot say when water arrives. On the seasonal example model, snapshots credit the well with west-edge water that, with transient tracking, does not arrive within 30 years (Section 4.4). <!-- README_method.md, Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §§2–4 --> Forward transient tracking also gives the share of pumped water that was stored in the aquifer at the start. This share matters where the stored water differs in quality from the water that replaces it. A GWT run with unit concentration in the source water gives the same result (Section 4.4).

Transient tracking still differs from a GWT run in three ways. It tracks every source in one run, with no transport model to build. It also runs backward in time, which gives the area whose water reaches the well within a stated horizon (Section 2.8). And it checks the water budget at every step. GWT adds dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022; Bedekar et al. 2024 [check: abstract only]), so we would use GWT where these matter.

Transient tracking needs a porosity. It also needs the budget of every time step to be saved (Section 7). Where porosity and specific yield differ, the code's two ways of computing a cell's water volume give different results (Section 2.7; {SI: transient checks on the seasonal example model}). We would compare both when a result is close to a decision threshold.

## 6.4 Drawing a protection zone

1. **Map F in the uppermost active cells, on cells of 50 m or less where the grid allows.** On Neversink, 96 % of the wells' water entered the model in these cells (Section 5.1). <!-- NS §2 Coverage; mp7_coverage.csv --> With cells of 50 m or less, the F > 0.5 area stayed within 5 % of the reference area in the synthetic tests (Section 4.3). <!-- DS §5; README_method.md Fig. 3 caption -->

2. **Choose the threshold by how much the zone may miss.** The F > 0.5 area holds the cells where most of the water ends at the well. On Neversink, almost all of its cells were published contributing cells (Section 5.2). <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.5: coverage 0.590, f_zone_inside_prt 0.917); NS §2, Table 1 (417 of 445 cells published, 43 % of the published area); NS §5, Table 3a --> Where the zone must err on the side of caution, draw the F > 0.1 area instead. It held the reference area in every synthetic test, at the cost of being larger (Section 4.3). <!-- DS §5, Summary --> On Neversink it still missed 27 % of the published area, partly at weak sinks and partly through mixing between sinks (Sections 5.2 and 5.3). <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.1: coverage 0.871, 89 missed); NS §2 (711 of 980 published contributing cells inside F > 0.1), §5, Table 3a -->

3. **Check the edge with particles where the area ends at a weak sink**, such as a buried stream cell. Run particles once stopping at these cells and once passing them. The difference shows how far the edge depends on that rule (Section 5.3; Fig. 7).

4. **Use gross accounting by default.** It credits each well with its pumping, as GWT transport does (Sections 2.3 and 4.1). One trace also covers every well (Section 2.4). Keep net accounting for reproducing FlowSource studies (Section 6.2).

5. **Choose the stream representation by the question.** Streams as cells answer which boundary supplies the well, so the area stops at the streams (Fig. 6a–c). The stream network answers where the water entered the model. On Neversink it traced all the wells' water to recharge. The area then extended upstream along the streams that feed the losing reaches (Section 5.4; Fig. 6d–f). In our judgement, a zone for controlling land use needs the stream network.

6. **Use backward tracking in time for a zone limited in time.** The mean travel time cannot set the edge of such a zone, because it is a mean over mixed water, not a first arrival (Section 2.6). For a steady model, repeat the steady flows over time steps that span the horizon. This has been checked only for long horizons, where it reproduces the steady F (Section 4.2). <!-- si/C.md (model 10 holds model 1's flows for 4 × 10⁵ d in 120 equal steps); verification_table.csv (m10_constant: 6.8 × 10⁻¹⁰) --> Short horizons need transport sub-steps (Section 3.2) and have not been tested. Until they are, we would draw short zones, such as 50-day inner zones, with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019) does. <!-- README_method.md Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §4 (30-year area from backward tracking in time); EA 2019 manual pp. 76–78 (SPZ1 50-day zone from MODPATH) -->

7. **For an ensemble, we would map the mean F over the realisations.** It correlated with the published probability of contribution. Unlike that probability, it follows the water reaching the wells rather than where particles stop in each realisation (Section 5.5). <!-- NS §6, Table 4; ensemble/probability_comparison.csv (r = 0.80); NS §6 (S region: published area CV 32 %, sum of F r = 0.00) -->

8. **Report with the zone** its accounting, threshold, stream representation and closure check, and the settings of any particle check (Sections 5.2–5.4).
