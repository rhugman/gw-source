# 6. Discussion

For transient flow the guidance below rests on synthetic models (§4.4), and for steady flow on one regional model whose published areas are a benchmark (§5).

## 6.1 Full mixing or particle tracking

On the same flow field, full mixing and weight-losing particles deliver the same water to the wells, within 2 %, by different paths (§5.3). <!-- NS §3, Table 3: 3,736 vs 3,671 m³/d, +1.8 %; prt_comparison.csv --> Full mixing gives exact budgets, the share each weak sink takes and many destinations from one trace, in 2 to 3 s on Neversink (§5.1); particles give advective paths and first arrival. <!-- NS Summary (Cost) --> For small zones, where the transition band of full mixing is a large part of the zone's width (§4.3), we would combine full mixing with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019) does for inner zones.

Particle conventions change the answer as much as the choice of method does. On overlap with the published cells, weight loss at weak sinks moves flow-weighted particles from 0.29 to 0.62, and the face that recharge and stream flows are assigned to moves particles from the published starts from 0.65 to 0.998 (Fig. 7). <!-- NS §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; mp7_reproduction.csv; prt_comparison.csv --> Each of these steps changes the overlap by about 0.33, as much as or more than the difference between weight-losing particles and full mixing (0.62 against 0.30 to 0.41). <!-- NS §3, Table 3; NS Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv --> Release depth changes the water that particles deliver by 35 % (2,413 against 3,736 m³/d), while full mixing and weight-losing particles agree on it within 2 %. <!-- NS §3, Table 3 --> A particle delineation used for a decision should therefore state its release position, stopping rule at weak sinks and face convention.

The threshold guidance holds less firmly on a regional model than in the synthetic tests. There, the F ≥ 0.1 area contained the reference zone in every run, missing at most 0.1 % of it, but was 8 to 90 % too large (§4.3). <!-- DS §5, Summary --> On Neversink, within the two caveats of §5.3, the F > 0.1 area misses 27 % of the published cells, whose particles stop at every weak sink, and 13 % of the PRT area (P > 0.5), whose particles do not {SI: net attribution and PRT-area maps}. <!-- NS §2 (711 of 980 published cells inside F > 0.1), §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv: coverage 0.871 --> The F > 0.5 area stays a core, 92 % of it inside the PRT area. <!-- NS §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv -->

## 6.2 Gross or net accounting

Net accounting brings the Neversink group total close to the published count, but the match does not hold per well. The sum of F rises from 910 to 978, against 980 published cells, and the overlap at F > 0.5 from 0.41 to 0.44 {SI: per-well gross and net}. <!-- NS §2, Table 2b; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv --> Gross and net accounting both fall short of the published count at the three wells that take all of their cell's outflow (about 410 against 514 cells). <!-- NS §2, Table 2b text; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv: gross 408, net 414 --> At the five weak-sink wells net accounting exceeds the published count only in sum (690 against 466 cells), with the 608 m³/d passing from U___7's cell into U___8's counted for both wells. <!-- NS §2, Tables 2a–2b text; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (689.7); net_vs_gross_wells.csv (dest_to_dest_flow 607.7) --> Per well it is above the published count at U___7 (214 against 58) and U___8 (249 against 163) but below it at SV_487 (94 against 97) and U__55 (97 against 116), while gross accounting is closer in sum (502 against 466). <!-- NS §2, Table 2b text; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv --> The group-level match is partly differences of both signs cancelling. One possible reading, not tested, is that stopping a particle at the first weak sink credits the well with all the water reaching its cell, as net accounting does; the per-well counts do not support it.

## 6.3 Snapshots, transient tracking or a GWT run

Snapshots say where a time step's water enters under that step's flows, in seconds and without porosity. Transient tracking says when the water arrives and how much of the pumped water was already stored. On tutorial 08, snapshots credit the well with 12 % west-edge inflow on average, while with transient tracking none arrives within 30 years, and 68 % of the water pumped over the 30 years was in the aquifer at the start (Fig. 4). <!-- README_method.md, Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §§2–4 --> Where the stored water differs in quality from the water replacing it, only transient tracking answers the question.

Transient tracking needs porosity and every time step saved (§7). Where porosity and specific yield differ, the two volume options differ (tutorial 08: 2 × 10⁻⁴ forward, 4 × 10⁻³ backward; §4.4); we would compare both when a result is close to a decision threshold. <!-- tutorial 08 §7: 1.9e-4 in c, 3.7e-3 in w --> With GWT's water volumes and time steps, forward transient tracking is a GWT unit-concentration run, to 9.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ on tutorial 08 (§4.4). <!-- tutorial 08 §8; Fig. 4 caption --> The code adds backward tracking in time, all tags at once in each step, and the checks; GWT adds dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022; Bedekar et al. 2024). We would use GWT where dispersion or reactions matter, and transient tracking where a well's catchment in time, or many sources at once, are needed.

## 6.4 Streams, ensembles and published benchmarks

How streams are represented is a choice of question: streams as cells answers "which package delivers the water", the stream network "where did the water enter" (§5.4). An area drawn with streams as cells stops at the streams, and a report should say so. In the S region of Neversink the published area follows PRT with the published conventions (r = 0.997) but not the sum of F (r = 0.00) (§5.5), so variation of a particle-tracked area between realisations need not reflect variation in the water reaching the wells. <!-- NS §6 -->

Two bookkeeping offsets in the published Monte Carlo contributing areas had to be inferred before the areas could be paired with their realisations (§5.5; {SI: Monte Carlo bookkeeping offsets}); archiving the steps that write such files would let later studies use them directly. <!-- NS §6, §8 -->

## 6.5 Drawing a protection zone

From these tests we would proceed as follows. We would take the F > 0.5 area as the single best estimate of the advective zone: with cells of 50 m or less it was within 5 % of the reference area in the synthetic tests (§4.3; Fig. 3), and on Neversink 92 % of it lay inside the PRT area (§5.3). <!-- DS §5; README_method.md Fig. 3 caption; NS §5, Table 3a --> We would draw the F ≥ 0.1 area as the wider area, although on Neversink it missed 13 to 27 % of the particle-tracked areas (§6.1). <!-- DS §5; NS §2, §5, Table 3a --> Where the area ends at weak sinks, such as gaining streams, we would check it with particle tracking under stated conventions (§5.3; Fig. 7). We would use gross accounting by default, since it credits each well with its pumping, treats sink terms as GWT does (§4.1) and covers every well in one trace (§2.3); net accounting we would keep for reproducing studies made with the reference method. For an area tied to a travel time, we would use backward tracking in time over the stated horizon rather than the mean travel time, which is a mean over mixed water, not a first arrival (§2.6). On tutorial 08 the 30-year area from backward tracking in time does not reach the west edge, while the steady F does (Fig. 4c–d; §4.4).
