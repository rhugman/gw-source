# 6. Discussion

For transient flow the guidance below rests on synthetic models (Section 4.4), and for steady flow on one regional model whose published areas are a benchmark (Section 5).

## 6.1 Full mixing or particle tracking

On the same flow field, full mixing and weight-losing particles deliver the same volume of water to the wells, within 2 %, by different paths (Section 5.3). <!-- NS §3, Table 3: 3,736 vs 3,671 m³/d, +1.8 %; prt_comparison.csv --> Full mixing gives exact budgets, the share each weak sink takes and many destinations from one trace, in 2–3 s on Neversink (Section 5.1); particles give advective paths and first arrival. <!-- NS Summary (Cost) --> For small protection zones, where the transition band of full mixing spans much of the width (Section 4.3), we would combine full mixing with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019, Appendix C4.3–C4.4) does for inner zones. <!-- EA 2019 manual, references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, pp. 76–78: MODPATH time-of-travel zones are the basis of SPZ1 (50-day) and SPZ2, total capture zones are checked against the full-mixing outputs, and the full-mixing code's own 50- and 400-day travel times come from one particle per cell -->

Particle conventions change the answer as much as the choice of method does. On overlap with the published contributing cells, weight loss at the other sinks moves flow-weighted particles from 0.29 to 0.62, and the face that recharge and stream flows are assigned to moves particles released at the published starts from 0.65 to 0.998 (Fig. 7). <!-- NS §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; mp7_reproduction.csv; prt_comparison.csv --> Each of these steps changes the overlap by 0.33–0.35, as much as or more than the difference between weight-losing particles and full mixing (0.62 against 0.30–0.41). <!-- NS §3, Table 3; NS Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv --> Release at the water table cuts the water particles deliver by 35 % (2,413 against 3,736 m³/d), while full mixing and weight-losing particles agree on it within 2 %. <!-- NS §3, Table 3 ("Release depth matters at the water table only"); prt_comparison.csv --> A particle delineation used for a decision should therefore state its release position, stopping rule at weak sinks and face convention.

## 6.2 Gross or net accounting

Net accounting brings the Neversink group total close to the published count, but not well by well. ## 6.3 Snapshots, transient tracking or a GWT run

Snapshots say where a time step's water enters under that step's flows, in seconds and without porosity. Transient tracking says when the water arrives and how much of the pumped water was already stored. On tutorial 08, snapshots credit the well with 12 % west-edge inflow on average, while with transient tracking none arrives within 30 years, and 68 % of the water pumped over the 30 years was in the aquifer at the start (Fig. 4). <!-- README_method.md, Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §§2–4 --> Where the stored water differs in quality from the water replacing it, transient tracking answers the question from one flow run, for every source at once, with no transport run.

Transient tracking needs porosity and every time step saved (Section 7). Where porosity and specific yield differ, the two volume options differ (Section 4.4); we would compare both when a result is close to a decision threshold. With GWT's water volumes and time steps, forward transient tracking is a GWT unit-concentration run to round-off (Section 4.4). The code adds backward tracking in time, all tags at once in each step, and the checks; GWT adds dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022; Bedekar et al. 2024 [check: abstract only]). We would use GWT where dispersion or reactions matter, and transient tracking where a well's catchment in time, or many sources at once, are needed.

## 6.4 Streams as cells or as a stream network

How streams are represented is a choice of question: streams as cells answers "which package delivers the water", the stream network "where did the water enter" (Section 5.4). An area drawn with streams as cells stops at the streams, and a report should say so. With the stream network the Neversink area extends upstream along the streams that feed the losing reaches (Section 5.4; Fig. 6d–f).

## 6.5 Drawing a protection zone

From these tests we would draw a protection zone as follows. The F > 0.5 area is the core area. With cells of 50 m or less it was within 5 % of the reference area in the synthetic tests (F ≥ 0.5; Section 4.3; Fig. 3). <!-- DS §5; README_method.md Fig. 3 caption --> On Neversink it held 59 % of the PRT area and 43 % of the published contributing cells, with 92 % of its cells inside the PRT area (Sections 5.2–5.3), so it marks a core, not the size of the area. <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.5: coverage 0.590, f_zone_inside_prt 0.917); NS §2, Table 1 (417 of 445 cells published, 43 % of the published area); NS §5, Table 3a --> The expected area, the sum of F over the start cells, gives the size: 910 cells against 980 published contributing cells (Section 5.2). <!-- NS §2, Table 1; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (priority 910.2) --> The F ≥ 0.1 area is the wider area. In the synthetic tests it contained the reference area in every run, missing at most 0.1 % of it, but was 8–90 % too large (Section 4.3). <!-- DS §5, Summary --> It is not a guaranteed envelope, and it can miss cells between sinks as well as at weak sinks: on Neversink the F > 0.1 area missed 13 % of the PRT area, whose particles lose weight at sinks as full mixing does, and 27 % of the published contributing cells, whose particles stop at every weak sink (Sections 5.2–5.3, within the caveats there). <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.1: coverage 0.871, 89 missed); NS §2 (711 of 980 published contributing cells inside F > 0.1), §5, Table 3a --> Where the area ends at weak sinks, such as gaining streams, a particle check under stated conventions shows how much the stopping rule matters (Section 5.3; Fig. 7). Gross accounting is the default, since it credits each well with its pumping, treats sink terms as GWT does (Section 4.1) and covers every well in one trace (Section 2.4; Table 1); net accounting serves to reproduce studies made with FlowSource. A time-limited area comes from backward tracking in time over the stated horizon, not from the mean travel time, which is a mean over mixed water, not a first arrival (Section 2.6). How streams are represented is a model choice that changes the area (Section 6.4; Fig. 6), and one model gives no basis for preferring either.
