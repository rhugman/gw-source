# 6. Discussion

The evidence here is limited in two ways. Apart from Neversink, which is steady state, the method is verified on synthetic models only, so the transient guidance rests on them (§4.2, §4.4). And the Neversink comparison is against a benchmark, not a reference: the published areas are one particle convention (§5.3). What follows is guidance for choosing a protection-zone method within that evidence, not general rules.

## 6.1 Full mixing or particle tracking

On the same flow field, full mixing and weight-losing particles deliver the same water to the wells, within 2 %, by different paths (§5.3). <!-- NS §3, Table 3: 3,736 vs 3,671 m³/d, +1.8 %; prt_comparison.csv --> Full mixing gives exact budgets, the share each weak sink takes and many destinations from one trace in seconds (§3.3); particles give advective paths and first arrival. Full mixing therefore suits budget questions (how much water, from which source, across an ensemble), and particles questions of path and arrival. For small zones, where the spreading of full mixing is comparable to the zone width, we would combine the F = 0.5 area with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019) does (§4.3).

Particle conventions change the answer as much as the choice of method does. On overlap with the published cells, weight loss at weak sinks moves flow-weighted particles from 0.29 to 0.62, and the face that recharge and stream flows are assigned to moves particles from the published starts from 0.65 to 0.998 (Table 3; Fig. 7). <!-- NS §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; mp7_reproduction.csv; prt_comparison.csv --> Each of these steps is about a third, as large as or larger than the step from weight-losing particles to full mixing (0.62 against 0.30 to 0.41). <!-- NS §3, Table 3; NS Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv --> Release depth changes the water that particles deliver by a third (2,413 against 3,736 m³/d), while full mixing and weight-losing particles agree on it within 2 %. <!-- NS §3, Table 3 --> A particle delineation used for a decision, or as a benchmark, should therefore state its release position, stopping rule at weak sinks and face convention.

The threshold guidance holds less firmly on a regional model than in the synthetic tests. There, the F ≥ 0.1 area contained the reference zone in every run (missing at most 0.1 % of its area), at the cost of an area 8 to 90 % too large (§4.3). <!-- DS §5, Summary --> On Neversink the F > 0.1 area misses 27 % of the published cells, whose particles stop at every weak sink, and 13 % of the weight-losing PRT zone P > 0.5, whose particles do not: 89 cells, carrying 6 % of the water PRT delivers to the wells (Fig. S1b–d; Table S6). <!-- NS §2 (711 of 980 published cells inside F > 0.1), §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv: coverage 0.871, 89 missed, 226.7 of 3,736 m³/d --> The F > 0.5 area stays a core, 92 % of it inside the PRT zone. <!-- NS §5, Table 3a; envelope_prt.csv --> Two caveats apply: the PRT reference shares full mixing at weak sinks, and it was seeded only where F > 10⁻⁶, so its 87 % coverage is an upper bound (§5.3). <!-- NS §5, Table 3a and caveats; envelope_prt_notes.csv --> We therefore present F ≥ 0.1 as a wide zone that contained the reference in the synthetic tests but not fully on Neversink, and F = 0.5 as an advective core.

## 6.2 Gross or net accounting

Net accounting brings the Neversink group total close to the published count, but the match does not hold per well. The sum of F rises from 910 to 978, against 980 published cells, and the overlap at F > 0.5 from 0.41 to 0.44; at F > 0.1 it is about the same (0.28 against 0.30) (Table S5). <!-- NS §2, Table 2b; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv --> Both modes fall short of the published count at the three wells that take all of their cell's outflow (about 410 against 514 cells). <!-- NS §2, Table 2b text; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv: gross 408, net 414 --> At the five weak-sink wells net accounting exceeds it (U___7 214 against 58 cells, U___8 249 against 163), while gross accounting is closer (502 against 466 in all). <!-- NS §2, Table 2b text; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv --> The group-level match is therefore partly differences of both signs cancelling. One possible reading, not tested, is that stopping a particle at the first weak sink credits the well with all the water reaching its cell, as net accounting does; the per-well counts do not support it. For a decision, the choice is what a well should be credited with (§2.3): its pumping, or all the water passing its cell, which on Neversink is 2.26 times the pumping at U___7 (§5.2; Fig. S1a). <!-- NS §2, Table 2a; net_vs_gross_wells.csv -->

## 6.3 Snapshots, transient tracking or a GWT run

This comparison rests on the one-layer synthetic model of tutorial 08 (§4.4). Snapshots say where a time step's water enters under that step's flows, in seconds and without porosity. Transient tracking says when the water arrives and how much of the pumped water was already stored. On tutorial 08, snapshots credit the well with 12 % west-edge inflow on average, while tracked, none arrives within 30 years, and 68 % of the water pumped over the 30 years was in the aquifer at the start (Fig. 4). <!-- docs/paper/figures/README_method.md, Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §§2–4 --> Where the stored water differs in quality from the water replacing it, only tracking answers the question. Tracking needs porosity and every time step saved (§7). Where porosity and specific yield differ, the two volume options differ (tutorial 08: 2 × 10⁻⁴ forward, 4 × 10⁻³ backward; §2.8). <!-- tutorial 08 §7: 1.9e-4 in c, 3.7e-3 in w --> We would compare both options when a result is close to a decision threshold.

With GWT's volumes and time steps, forward tracking is a GWT unit-concentration run, to 9.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ on tutorial 08 (§4.4). <!-- tutorial 08 §8; Fig. 4 caption --> The choice is not one of accuracy. The post-processor adds backward tracking in time, all tags in one solve per step, and the checks; GWT adds dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022; Bedekar et al. 2024). Use GWT where dispersion or reactions matter, and tracking where a well's catchment in time or many sources at once are needed.

## 6.4 Streams, ensembles and published benchmarks

How streams are represented is a choice of question: streams as cells answers "which package delivers the water", the stream network "where did the water enter" (§5.4). A zone drawn with streams as cells stops at the streams, and a report should say so. With streams as cells, the fall in stream share as pumping rises explains most of the faster growth of the Neversink area (r = 0.94 across realisations; Fig. 10). <!-- NS §7; scenarios/source_table.csv; figures/README.md Fig. 10 -->

Variation of a particle-tracked area between realisations can come from the particle conventions rather than from the water reaching the wells: in the S region of Neversink the published area follows PRT with the published conventions (r = 0.997) but not the sum of F (r = 0.00) (§5.5). <!-- NS §6 -->

Last, on reusing published benchmarks: two bookkeeping offsets in the published Monte Carlo contributing areas, a one-row shift and a text-sorted run order, had to be inferred before the areas could be paired with their realisations (§5.5; Text S4). <!-- NS §6, §8 --> Archiving the steps that write such files would let later studies use them directly.
