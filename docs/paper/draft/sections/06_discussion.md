# 6. Discussion

Three outcomes frame this discussion. On a published regional model, full mixing gives contributing areas within the range that particle tracking gives under different particle settings on the same flow field (Section 5). <!-- NS §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; prt_comparison.csv, mp7_reproduction.csv (PRT 0.29–0.998); NS §2, Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv (full mixing 0.30–0.41) --> The water-budget checks hold exactly, to the computer's precision, on every test model and, for closure, on every Neversink trace (Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 5.1). <!-- verification_table.md, largest error per check; NS §1, §4; checks_cells.csv, checks_sfr_network.csv --> Transient tracking answers questions that snapshots cannot, such as how much of the pumped water was already stored in the aquifer (Section 4.4). The subsections discuss what these outcomes mean in practice.

## 6.1 Full mixing or particle tracking

The first choice is between full mixing and particle tracking. Full mixing assumes that the water in each cell is fully mixed, so every outflow carries the same mix of origins. On the Neversink flow field, full mixing and weight-losing particles delivered the same volume of water to the wells, within 2 % (Section 5.3). <!-- NS §3, Table 3: 3,736 vs 3,671 m³/d, +1.8 %; prt_comparison.csv --> Weight-losing particles give up, at each sink they pass, the share of their flow that the sink takes, as full mixing does. Full mixing gives exact budgets and many destinations from one trace, in 2–3 s on Neversink (Section 5.1). <!-- NS Summary (Cost) --> It also gives the share of water taken by each weak sink, a well or stream that takes only part of the water passing its cell. Particles give what full mixing cannot, flow paths and first arrival times.

These strengths suggest a division of work for small protection zones. Mixing whole cells blurs the edge of an area into a transition band of cells where F, the share of a cell's water that ends at the well, lies between 0.1 and 0.9 (Section 4.3). In a small zone this band spans much of the width. For such zones we would combine full mixing with particle tracking, as the Environment Agency (2019, Appendix C4.3–C4.4) does for inner zones. <!-- EA 2019 manual, references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, pp. 76–78: MODPATH time-of-travel zones are the basis of SPZ1 (50-day) and SPZ2, total capture zones are checked against the full-mixing outputs, and the full-mixing code's own 50- and 400-day travel times come from one particle per cell -->

A particle check raises its own question, which settings to use. On Neversink, the settings changed the area as much as the choice of method did (Section 5.3). Agreement was measured as the overlap, the share of cells two areas have in common. Two settings each moved the overlap with the published Neversink contributing cells, those whose published particle ends in a supply well, by 0.33–0.35 (Fig. 7). One is whether particles lose weight at sinks other than the wells. The other is which cell face recharge and stream flows enter through. <!-- NS §3, Table 3 and reproduction table; mp7_reproduction.csv; prt_comparison.csv --> Each moved the overlap as much as or more than the switch from weight-losing particles to full mixing. <!-- NS §3, Table 3; NS Table 1; agreement_with_mp7.csv --> Releasing particles at the water table cut the water they delivered by 35 %. <!-- NS §3, Table 3 ("Release depth matters at the water table only"); prt_comparison.csv --> A particle delineation used for a decision should therefore state its release position, its stopping rule at weak sinks and the cell face it assigns boundary flows to.

## 6.2 Gross or net accounting

The second choice is how to credit a weak-sink well. Gross accounting, the default, credits the well with what it pumps (Section 2.3). Net accounting, as in FlowSource, credits it with all the water that passes through its cell. On Neversink, net accounting raised the sum of F, the expected number of contributing cells, from 910 to 978, against 980 published contributing cells. <!-- NS §2, Table 2b; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (priority 910.2, 978.5; jaccard_F>0.5 0.41, 0.44) -->

The match does not hold well by well. Net accounting exceeds the published count at three wells and falls below it at the other five ({SI: per-well gross and net}). <!-- NS §2, Tables 2a–2b; evidence: si/F_editor.md {SI: per-well gross and net} table (net against published: SV_407 37/32, U___7 214/58, U___8 249/163 above; SV_193 26/48, SV_227 74/115, SV_487 94/97, U_917 314/351, U__55 97/116 below); net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (sum of net expected_cells 1,103.6); net_vs_gross_wells.csv (dest_to_dest_flow 607.7, dest_onward_to_dest 70.1) --> The group-level match is therefore partly differences of both signs cancelling. It is no reason to prefer net accounting.

## 6.3 Snapshots, transient tracking or a GWT run

For transient models the choice is between snapshots, transient tracking and a GWT transport run. A snapshot traces one time step as if its flows were steady. Storage release then counts as a source of unknown origin. Snapshots take seconds and need no porosity. Transient tracking instead carries tagged water from step to step through the water stored in each cell (Section 2.7). On the seasonal example model (Section 4.4), a synthetic one-layer aquifer, snapshots credit the well with 12 % west-edge inflow on average. With transient tracking, no west-edge water reaches the well within 30 years. Of the water pumped over the 30 years, 68 % was in the aquifer at the start (Fig. 4). <!-- README_method.md, Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §§2–4 --> This share matters where the stored water differs in quality from the water replacing it.

Transient tracking needs porosity and every time step saved (Section 7). Where porosity and specific yield differ, the code's two ways of computing a cell's water volume give different results (Section 4.4). We would compare both when a result is close to a decision threshold.

GWT can answer some of the same questions. With GWT's water volumes and time steps, forward transient tracking reproduces exactly a GWT run that gives the source water a concentration of 1 (Section 4.4). The code adds backward tracking in time, which gives the fraction of each cell's water that later leaves through the well (Section 2.8). GWT adds dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022; Bedekar et al. 2024 [check: abstract only]). We would use GWT where dispersion or reactions matter.

## 6.4 Streams as cells or as a stream network

How streams are represented decides which question a trace answers (Section 5.4). With streams as cells, stream leakage is a source of the aquifer cell. Tracing then stops at the stream, showing which package delivers the water. With the stream network, stream reaches become extra cells linked by stream routing. Tracing then follows leakage up the stream to where its water entered the model. On Neversink this extends the area upstream along the streams that feed the losing reaches (Fig. 6d–f).

## 6.5 Drawing a protection zone

Five pieces of advice follow for a modeller drawing a protection zone with full mixing.

1. **Draw the cells where more than half the water ends at the well (F > 0.5) as the cells most certainly in the zone.** This area changed little with cell size in the synthetic tests and lay almost wholly inside both particle-tracked areas on Neversink (Sections 4.3 and 5.2–5.3). <!-- DS §5; README_method.md Fig. 3 caption --> <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.5: coverage 0.590, f_zone_inside_prt 0.917); NS §2, Table 1 (417 of 445 cells published, 43 % of the published area); NS §5, Table 3a --> Take the size of the zone from the expected area, the sum of F, since the F > 0.5 area is smaller (Section 5.2). <!-- NS §2, Table 1; net_vs_gross_mp7.csv (priority 910.2) --> Where the decision calls for caution, draw the wider area where more than one tenth of the water ends at the well (F > 0.1). It held the reference area in every synthetic test, at the cost of being larger (Section 4.3). <!-- DS §5, Summary --> It is not a guaranteed envelope, because on Neversink it missed some cells next to weak sinks and between sinks (Sections 5.2–5.3). <!-- envelope_prt.csv (all, P>0.5, F>0.1: coverage 0.871, 89 missed); NS §2 (711 of 980 published contributing cells inside F > 0.1), §5, Table 3a -->

2. **Add a particle check where the area ends at a weak sink, such as a buried stream cell.** A buried stream cell is a stream reach with an active aquifer cell above it. A check under stated settings shows how far the rule to stop or pass particles there moves the edge (Section 5.3; Fig. 7). Add one too where the zone is only a few cells wide (Section 6.1).

3. **Use gross accounting by default.** It credits each well with its pumping, as GWT transport does (Sections 2.3 and 4.1). One trace also covers every well (Section 2.4; Table 1). Use net accounting only to reproduce studies made with FlowSource (Section 6.2).

4. **State the stream representation.** The choice changes the area, which stops at the streams when they are treated as cells (Section 6.4; Fig. 6). One model gives no basis for preferring either.

5. **Use transient tracking for a zone limited in time, or where the share of stored water matters (Section 6.3).** A time-limited zone needs backward tracking in time over the stated horizon. The mean travel time cannot set its edge, because it is a mean over mixed water, not a first arrival (Section 2.6). On the seasonal example model, the 30-year area stops short of the west edge, unlike the steady area (Fig. 4c–d). <!-- README_method.md Fig. 4 caption; tutorial 08 §4 --> For a steady model, repeat the steady flows over time steps that span the horizon. This is checked only for long horizons, where it reproduces the steady F (Section 4.2). <!-- si/C.md (model 10 holds model 1's flows for 4 × 10⁵ d in 120 equal steps); verification_table.csv (m10_constant: 6.8 × 10⁻¹⁰) --> Short horizons, which need transport sub-steps (Section 4.4), were not tested.
