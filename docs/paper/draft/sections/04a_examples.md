# 4. Worked examples

Each example in this section answers one question about a supply well. All of them start from one small example model, so that results can be compared from one example to the next. The model is a single unconfined layer, 2 km × 2 km, made of 40 × 40 cells of 50 m. Its top is at 20 m and its bottom at −30 m. The hydraulic conductivity is 5 m/d. Recharge of 5 × 10⁻⁴ m/d falls on every cell. A constant head of 10 m along the west edge brings in regional groundwater. A river with a stage of 6 m runs north–south through column 31. A supply well pumps 800 m³/d from row 21, column 29, 100 m west of the river. ## 4.1 Where does the well's water come from?

A protection zone starts from the land whose water reaches the well. This example traces the well backward on the unchanged shared model. The trace gives F in every cell. F is the share of a cell's water that ends at the well (Section 2.2).

Fig. 2a maps F. The contributing area runs from the well back to the west edge. Cells with F > 0.5 send more than half their water to the well. They cover 1.143 km². Cells with F > 0.1 send more than a tenth. They cover 1.575 km². <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §5, cell 16 (F > 0.1: 630 cells, 1.575 km²; F > 0.5: 457 cells, 1.143 km²) --> Between the two contours, full mixing spreads the area's edge over a few cells. A narrow tail of F also crosses the river. In this one-layer model the river cells are weak sinks (Section 1). Full mixing passes the water they do not take on toward the well. A threshold-free measure of size is the sum over all cells of F times the cell area. We refer to it as the expected area. Here it is 1.173 km². <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §5, cell 16 (sum of F × cell area 1.173 km²); cell 15 (tail east of the river: weak-sink river cells in one layer) -->

The same trace splits the pumped water by source (Fig. 2c). Of the 800 m³/d, 71 % is recharge, 15 % enters at the west edge and 14 % leaks from the river. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §6, cell 20 (recharge 568.6 m³/d, 0.711; west edge 118.1, 0.148; river 113.3, 0.142) --> The river share is the number a study of riverbank filtration would need. The traced sources add up to the 800 m³/d pumped, as the closure check requires (Section 2.5). <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §7, cell 22 (800.0000 against 800.0000 m³/d, relative closure error 0.0) --> That check tests the solve but not the position of the area's edge.

Particle tracking with PRT answers the same question by another method, on the same flows. Particles start from every source term in cells with F > 0. Each particle carries a share of its source's flow as a weight. It loses weight at every other sink it passes, in proportion to the flow that sink takes. We refer to these as weight-losing particles. The particle share of a cell is the weight that reaches the well over the weight released there. The particles deliver 799.9 of the 800 m³/d pumped. That total says little about the area, because particles that conserve water must deliver what the well pumps. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (18,208 particles from 1,133 cells; 799.9 of 800.0 m³/d) --> The cells with a particle share above 0.5 cover 1.168 km². The F > 0.5 area is 2.1 % smaller. The F > 0.1 area is 35 % larger and contains every cell of the particle area. <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (P > 0.5: 467 cells, 1.168 km²; F > 0.5 −2.1 %, 14 particle cells outside; F > 0.1 +34.9 %, 0 particle cells outside) --> Cell by cell, the particle share and F differ by 0.058 on average. In the 372 cells with F between 0.05 and 0.95, they differ by 0.164 on average (Fig. 2b). <!-- evidence: tutorials/01_quickstart/01_quickstart.ipynb §8, cell 24 (mean |P − F| 0.058 over 1,133 release cells, 0.164 over 372 edge cells) --> In those cells, most particles either reach the well or miss it. Full mixing gives the cell an in-between value.

Because full mixing spreads the edge, the threshold on F that draws an area is a choice. Tests on two simple problems compared full-mixing areas with reference areas drawn analytically or with particles ({SI: numerical dispersion}). On cells of 50 m or less, the F > 0.5 area was within 5 % of the size of the reference area. On cells of 12.5 to 50 m, the F > 0.1 area missed at most 0.1 % of the reference area. It was 8 to 90 % larger. Section 6 discusses how to choose. <!-- evidence: docs/dispersion_study.md Summary and §7 (F ≥ 0.5 within 5 % at h ≤ 50 m, relative to the analytical zone (problem A) or fine-grid PRT (problem B); F ≥ 0.1 contained the whole reference zone in every run, missed ≤ 0.1 %; 30 to 90 % too large for problem A and 8 to 16 % for problem B at h = 12.5 to 50 m); si/C.md, {SI: numerical dispersion maps and time steps}, paragraph on strict and non-strict thresholds (no full-mixing cell has F exactly 0.1 or 0.5, so the study's F ≥ 0.1 and F ≥ 0.5 select the same cells as F > 0.1 and F > 0.5; the SI says so) -->

## 4.2 How much water should a small well be credited with?

A modeller who updates a protection zone first drawn with FlowSource needs to know where gross and net accounting differ (Section 2.4). They differ only at weak sinks. A small well in strong regional flow is a weak sink. Its cell passes most of its water on to the neighbouring cells. This example shows how large the difference can be. The shared model is split into three layers, of which the middle one is the least permeable ({SI: example and test models}). The supply well pumps 800 m³/d from layer 3. A second, small well pumps 5 m³/d from layer 3 at row 17, column 13, upgradient of the supply well. <!-- evidence: tutorials/herebedragons.py LAYER_BOTM (0, −12, −30 m), LAYER_K (10, 1, 15 m/d), k33_ratio 0.1, LAYERED_WELLS (supply_well layer 3, row 21, column 29, −800 m³/d; weak_well layer 3, row 17, column 13, −5 m³/d); tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 13 -->

The small well's cell carries 33.7 m³/d. The well takes 5 m³/d of it. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 13 (inflow 33.67 m³/d, taken by the well 5.00, passed on 28.67) --> Gross accounting credits the well with these 5 m³/d. F in its cell is then 5/33.7 = 0.149. Net accounting fixes F = 1 in the cell. It credits the well with all 33.7 m³/d, 6.7 times what it pumps. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 15 (weak_well: F in cell 0.149 gross, 1.0 net; 5.0 against 33.667 m³/d; net / gross 6.733) --> Both accountings find the same flow path, a narrow strip along row 17 from the west edge to the well (Fig. 3b, c). Under net accounting, 93–99 % of the water in layer 3 along that strip is credited to the small well. Under gross accounting, 82 % of the water in the small well's cell flows on to the supply well. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cell 18 (net F along row 17 west of the well, layer 3: 0.93 to 0.99; gross F to the supply well in the weak well's cell 0.821) --> The supply well takes all the water that enters its cell. For this well the two accountings agree. <!-- evidence: tutorials/02_accounting/02_accounting.ipynb §3, cells 14 and 15 (supply_well: cell throughflow 800.0 m³/d, pumping 800.0; F in the well cell 1.0 gross and net) --> Because net accounting credits a weak sink with water it does not pump, we use it only to reproduce studies made with FlowSource.

## 4.3 Where does each source's water go?

Some questions start at a source rather than at the well. A water-quality study may ask how much river water, or how much recharge from one field, reaches the well. A forward trace answers it. It gives G in every cell. G is the share of a cell's water that came from the source (Section 2.2). The model is the shared model, unchanged.

River water enters the aquifer only through the reaches in rows 19 to 23, where pumping draws the water table below the river stage. From there it spreads to the well (Fig. 4a). <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §4, cell 12 (losing river reaches in rows 19 to 23) --> All 113 m³/d that the river loses reaches the well. Of the regional inflow at the west edge, 37 % reaches the well. Of the recharge, 29 % reaches the well and 71 % drains to the river (Fig. 4c). <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §5, cell 14 (river 113.3 of 113.3 m³/d to the well; west edge 118.1 of 323.9, 0.365; recharge 568.6 of 1,950.0, 0.292; 1,381.4 to the river) --> G in the well cell gives the pumped water by source. The shares are 14 % river water, 15 % regional inflow and 71 % recharge, as the backward trace of Section 4.1 found. <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb §3, cell 10 (G in the well cell: river 0.142, west edge 0.148, recharge 0.711) -->

A source can also be one zone. Here it is the recharge on a 300 m × 300 m field in rows 11 to 16 and columns 13 to 18. The field receives 45 m³/d of recharge. Of this, 39 % reaches the well and the rest drains to the river (Fig. 4b). <!-- evidence: tutorials/03_forward/03_forward.ipynb Settings cell 2 (FIELD_ROWS (10, 16), FIELD_COLS (12, 18), 0-based, end exclusive) and §6, cell 16 (36 recharge cells, 45.0 m³/d; 17.7 m³/d to the well, 39 %; 27.3 m³/d to the river) --> For a solute that does not react, G is the ratio of the concentration in a cell to the concentration at the source. The forward–backward check requires the volume from each source to the well to be the same from either direction (Section 2.5). Here the two directions agree to better than 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³/d. ## 4.4 Does it work on my grid?

Real models seldom use a regular grid of square cells. Many use Voronoi grids refined around wells and rivers (DISV) or fully unstructured grids (DISU). Some use the XT3D option of MODFLOW 6 for anisotropy. This example builds the shared model on four grids. The first is the regular grid (DIS). The second is a Voronoi grid of 1,322 cells about 50 m across (DISV). The last two hold the regular cells written as DISU, with and without cell vertices. The same lines of code trace the well on each grid. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §1, cell 4 and §2, cell 9 (1,600, 1,322, 1,600 and 1,600 cells; closure error 0.0 on every grid) -->

The two DISU models have the same cells and connections as the DIS model. Their F is identical to the DIS result in every cell. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §4, cell 15 (max |F − F_DIS| 0.0e+00 for both DISU models); §9, cell 45 (assert atol 1e-12) --> Without cell vertices, a DISU model gives fractions and volumes but no maps or GIS polygons. PRT runs only on DIS and DISV grids. A particle check of the edge is therefore not available on DISU grids. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb (DISU without vertices: no maps); previous/07_limitations.md:13 (PRT supports DIS and DISV only) --> The Voronoi model is a different model of the same aquifer (Fig. 5a, b). Its contributing area has the same shape and reach. Its expected area is 8 % smaller, 1.082 against 1.173 km². The river supplies 21 % of the pumped water instead of 14 %. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §4, cell 15 (expected area 1.082 against 1.173 km², −7.8 %; river share 0.213 against 0.142) --> On the Voronoi grid, the nearest river cell centre is 70 m from the well's cell centre instead of 100 m. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §4, cell 17 (100.0 m on DIS, 70.0 m on DISV) --> We read the larger river share as a result of this shorter distance. We did not test this reading by changing the distance alone.

XT3D computes the flow across a face from the heads of the surrounding cells as well. Flow can then run against the head difference between two cells. Water can go round in flow loops (Section 2.2). The example uses a heterogeneous hydraulic conductivity with 20:1 anisotropy. The axes of the anisotropy rotate across the model. The trace found five groups of cells with flow loops, 40 cells in all (Fig. 5c). Together they circulate 4.4 m³/d. That is 0.016 % of the summed outflow of all cells in the model. <!-- evidence: tutorials/04_grids/04_grids.ipynb §6, cells 23 and 27 (solver block, 5 strongly connected sets of 4 to 15 cells; 40 of 1,600 cells; circulation 4.42 m³/d in all; circulating throughflow 9.90 + 10.63 + 1.20 + 0.19 + 0.02 = 21.9 m³/d, 8.0e-4 of the model throughflow, so the model throughflow (sum of cell outflows, src/gwsource/diagnostics.py cycle_report) is about 27,400 m³/d and 4.42 m³/d is 0.016 % of it; review alt_r1 F1: the 0.08 % belongs to the circulating throughflow, not to the circulation) --> The code solves the cells of each group together as one small block. ## 4.5 How does the contributing area change through the seasons?

Recharge is highest in winter. Many supply wells pump most in summer. So the contributing area moves through the year. This example makes the shared model transient. It adds storage, with a specific yield of 0.2 and a specific storage of 1 × 10⁻⁵ m⁻¹. Recharge is scaled by 1.8, 1.0, 0.1 and 1.1 in winter, spring, summer and autumn. The well pumps 640, 800, 1,120 and 640 m³/d in the same seasons. After a 1-day steady-state start, the model runs for 3 years, with three steps of about 30 days in each season. <!-- evidence: tutorials/herebedragons.py build_transient (sy 0.2, ss 1e-5), SEASON_RECHARGE (1.8, 1.0, 0.1, 1.1), SEASON_PUMPING (0.8, 1.0, 1.4, 0.8), seasonal_periods; tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §1, cell 4 (640, 800, 1,120, 640 m³/d; 37 saved steps; step lengths 1.0 and 30.44 d) --> Each of the 36 seasonal steps is traced as a snapshot (Section 2.7).

Fig. 6a–d maps F in the middle step of each season of the last year. The F > 0.5 area is smallest in winter, at 0.59 km². In winter the well pumps least. The rising water table also puts water into storage. Storage then acts as a sink that takes part of each cell's water. In summer the F > 0.5 area grows to 1.35 km². Its western end moves 350 m, out to the west edge. <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §3, cell 6 (F > 0.5 area: winter 0.592, summer 1.350 km²; west end x 375 m in winter, 25 m in summer, change −350 m) --> The well's sources change too. In summer, water released from storage supplies 52.5 % of the pumped water. In winter, recharge supplies 91 %. <!-- evidence: tutorials/05_transient_snapshots/05_transient_snapshots.ipynb §4, cell 9 (mean share by season: summer storage 0.525; winter recharge 0.911) -->
