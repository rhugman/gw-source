# 1. Introduction

Protection zones around supply wells and springs are drawn from the area that contributes water to them, usually from a groundwater flow model. Risk-based studies delineate the area across an ensemble, so that protection can be chosen by risk tolerance (Fienen et al. 2022). Modellers need to know how much water from each cell reaches the well and where its water comes from, with answers that agree with the water budget and can be repeated cheaply for every realisation and scenario.

Particle tracking (Pollock 1988, 2016) is the standard tool, and one forward run serves every well. A weak sink is a cell whose sink term, such as a well or a gaining stream, takes only part of the cell's outflow; a particle that enters it must either stop or pass, and the choice changes the area. In transient models, particles do not directly give the share of pumped water that was in the aquifer at the start.

Full-mixing methods work from the cell-by-cell flows instead and treat each cell as fully mixed, so all water leaving it carries the same mix of origins. The destination is the set of sink terms traced to, such as a well's pumping; the source is the set of source terms traced from. Backward, they give the fraction of each cell's water that leaves the model through the destination; forward, the fraction that came from the source. A contributing area is the set of cells whose backward fraction exceeds a threshold; the expected area is the sum of the fractions, in cells. These fractions are not capture fractions, the share of a well's pumping supplied by increased recharge or reduced discharge (Leake et al. 2010; Barlow et al. 2018); they follow water along the flows of a single run.

MODALL computes such fractions for steady models (Potter et al. 2008); McLane and Cecan (2009) [check: not read] discuss its spreading on coarse grids. The full-mixing method of the FlowSource program (Foley and Black 2013; Black and Foley 2013) sorts the cells in flow order and computes the fractions in one pass; it is used for source protection zones in England (Environment Agency 2019). Hydraulic mixing cells step fractions forward in time in stream cells (Partington et al. 2011), then overland cells (Partington et al. 2013) and later aquifer cells (Schilling et al. 2017 [check: not read]; Nogueira et al. 2022).

The unit-concentration method gives source water concentration 1 and other water 0, and reads the concentration as the source's share (Bedekar et al. 2024 [check: abstract only]); in MODFLOW 6 this runs in the Groundwater Transport (GWT) Model (Langevin et al. 2022). Forward tracking through storage is not new: hydraulic mixing cells do it explicitly and GWT implicitly. We found no method in Table 1 that tracks water backward in time; for unit-concentration transport we read only the abstract of Bedekar et al. (2024). Adjoint methods run backward in time for concentrations in continuous transport; we track water fractions on the flow model's time steps. Nor does any method in Table 1 check that the volume from a source to a destination is the same traced either way, and we found no published comparison of a flux-based delineation with particle-tracked contributing areas on a large real model.

We write full mixing as one sparse linear system per direction, solved exactly, including where flows form loops, with gross accounting by default (Section 2.3) and exact identities that check every trace against the water budget. For transient models we keep the per-step treatment, track water forward through storage as GWT does, and add backward tracking in time (Section 2.9) with its own forward–backward check. The open code reads MODFLOW 6 Groundwater Flow (GWF) output for every grid type, multi-model simulations and package networks such as Streamflow Routing (SFR) reaches. We ask three questions:

1. Can full mixing be written exactly, steady and transient, and checked against the water budget (Sections 2 and 4)?
2. Is it cheap enough for a regional model and an ensemble (Sections 3 and 5)?
3. What do the differences from a published particle-tracked benchmark consist of, and what do they mean for how a protection zone is drawn and reported (Sections 5 and 6)?
