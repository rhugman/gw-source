# 1. Introduction

Protection zones around supply wells and springs are drawn from the area that contributes water to them, usually taken from a groundwater flow model. In England, source protection zones are set by travel time to the source and by its whole catchment (Environment Agency 2019); risk-based workflows delineate the contributing area across an ensemble of models, so that protection can be chosen by risk tolerance (Fienen et al. 2022). A modeller then needs to know, cell by cell, how much water reaches the well and where the well's water comes from, with answers that agree with the model's water budget and are cheap enough to repeat for every realisation or pumping scenario.

Particle tracking (Pollock 1988, 2016) is the standard tool, but three situations are hard to handle with it. At a weak sink, a cell whose well takes only part of the cell's outflow, a particle must either stop or pass, and the choice changes the area. In transient models, backward particle tracking through time-varying flows is awkward (Environment Agency 2019). And each destination needs its own particles, so analyses of many wells or whole models become expensive (Black and Foley 2013).

"Capture" also has a water-budget meaning. The capture fraction of Leake et al. (2010) and Barlow et al. (2018) is the share of a well's pumping supplied by increased recharge or reduced discharge, for example streamflow depletion, found by comparing runs with and without pumping. The fractions in this paper follow water along the flows of a single run, so we reserve "capture fraction" for the budget sense.

Flux-based methods work from the model's cell-by-cell flows instead. Under full mixing each cell is fully mixed, so all water leaving it carries the same mix of origins. The destination is the sink term or terms traced to, such as a well's pumping; the source is the source term or terms traced from, such as recharge. Backward, the method gives the fraction of each cell's water that leaves the model through the destination; forward, the fraction that came from the source. A contributing area is the set of cells whose backward fraction exceeds a threshold, or the expected area, the sum of the fractions, in cells.

MODALL computes such fractions for steady models (Potter et al. 2008); McLane and Cecan (2009) discuss its spreading on coarse grids. The full-mixing method of Foley and Black (2013) and Black and Foley (2013), called the reference method here, sorts the cells in flow order and computes the fractions in one pass, and is used for the English zones (Environment Agency 2019). Hydraulic mixing cells step fractions forward in time, first in the stream cells of an integrated surface water–groundwater model (Partington et al. 2011), then also in overland cells (Partington et al. 2013) and later in aquifer cells (Schilling et al. 2017 [check: not read]; Nogueira et al. 2022).

In the unit-concentration method, source water is given concentration 1 and all other water 0, and the concentration is read as the source's share (Bedekar et al. 2024); in MODFLOW 6 this runs in the GWT Model (Langevin et al. 2022). Particle source apportionment uses cell budgets for mixing ratios in weak-source cells (Pryet et al. 2022). Backward in time, adjoint methods give the probability that water at a well came from each location and time (Neupauer and Wilson 1999), and groundwater age and life expectancy are Eulerian measures of travel time (Goode 1996; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006); all three are written for continuous transport.

The reference method is described in conference papers, slides and a poster (Foley and Black 2013; Black and Foley 2013; Black et al. 2021; Daley et al. 2022); we found no peer-reviewed description and no open code. As published, it deletes the smallest-flow connection in each flow loop, credits a weak-sink well with all the water passing its cell, and treats each time step of a transient model as a steady state, with no water carried between steps; the changes in its 2020 version are not documented. Forward tracking through storage is not new: hydraulic mixing cells do it explicitly, GWT implicitly, and the reference method's forward mode has been described as an Eulerian transport tool (Black et al. 2021). None of the methods we read tracks water backward in time (for unit-concentration transport, on an abstract and the authors' slides only; Bedekar et al. 2023, 2024), and none checks that the volume from a source to a destination is the same traced either way. Nor has a flux-based delineation been compared with particle-tracked contributing areas on a large real model.

We write full mixing as one sparse linear system per direction, solved exactly with or without flow loops, with gross accounting by default and the reference method's accounting as an option. Exact identities check every result against the water budget. For transient models we keep the per-step treatment, add forward tracking through storage, and add backward tracking in time, run from the end of the simulation towards its start, with its own forward–backward check. The open code reads MODFLOW 6 grids of every type, multi-model simulations and package networks. We ask three questions:

1. Can full mixing be written exactly, for steady and transient models, so that every result is checked against the water budget (Sections 2 and 4)?
2. Is it cheap enough for a regional model and an ensemble (Sections 3 and 5)?
3. On a published benchmark, what do the differences from a particle-tracked delineation consist of?
