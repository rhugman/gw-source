# 1. Introduction

Protection zones around supply wells and springs restrict activities that could pollute the water they supply (Environment Agency 2019). Each zone is drawn from the well's contributing area, the land whose water ends at the well. Risk-based studies compute this area for every realisation of a model ensemble, so that managers can choose protection by their tolerance of risk (Fienen et al. 2022). Such studies need the share of each cell's water that reaches the well. The answers must agree with the water budget. They must also be cheap to repeat for every realisation.

Four families of methods give these answers. Particle tracking follows water particles along flow paths. Full-mixing flow tracing works from the flows between fully mixed cells. Transport with tagged water follows one source's water through time. Adjoint and age methods work backward in time or on travel times.

Particle tracking (Pollock 1988, 2016) is the standard tool for contributing areas. Particles can also give the sources of a sink's water (Pryet et al. 2022). <!-- evidence: docs/paper/outline.md §1 (flux-based alternatives, particle source apportionment [PRY22]); references/SUMMARY.md §5.3 --> But particles need a rule at weak sinks. A weak sink is a cell whose sink, such as a well or a gaining stream, takes only part of the cell's water. A particle cannot split, so it must stop there or pass on. If it stops, all its water is credited to the sink. If it passes, none is. The rule chosen changes the contributing area (Section 5.3). In transient models, particles also do not directly give the share of pumped water that was already stored in the aquifer.

Full-mixing flow tracing needs no such rule. Each cell is treated as fully mixed. All water leaving a cell then carries the same mix of origins. A weak sink takes its share of each origin's water and passes the rest on. Traced backward from a destination, such as a well, full mixing gives the backward fraction F: the share of a cell's water that ends there. Traced forward from a source, such as recharge, it gives the forward fraction G: the share of a cell's water that came from it. A contributing area is the set of cells where F exceeds a threshold, such as one half. F is not the capture fraction. The capture fraction is the share of a well's pumping supplied by changes in other budget terms, such as reduced stream discharge, from two runs with and without pumping (Leake et al. 2010; Barlow et al. 2018). F is the share of each cell's water that ends at the well, from one run.

MODALL and FlowSource compute full-mixing fractions from MODFLOW flows. MODALL computes them for steady models (Potter et al. 2008). McLane and Cecan (2009) showed that its fractions spread more widely on coarser grids, a spread that is not hydrodynamic dispersion. FlowSource (Foley and Black 2013; Black and Foley 2013) sorts the cells from upstream to downstream and computes the fractions in one pass. It is used for source protection zones in England (Environment Agency 2019). As published, it handles three cases in its own way (Table 1). Where water circulates between cells, for example around a pair of wells, no cell order is purely downstream. FlowSource deletes the smallest-flow connection in each such flow loop (Section 2.4). It credits a weak-sink well with all the water passing its cell, which we call net accounting (Section 2.3). It traces each step of a transient model as a steady state, a snapshot, with nothing carried between steps. FlowSource is not open source.

Transport with tagged water does carry water from step to step. The unit-concentration method gives the source water a concentration of 1 and all other water 0. A cell's concentration is then the share of its water from the source (Bedekar et al. 2024 [check: abstract only]). In MODFLOW 6 this runs in the Groundwater Transport (GWT) Model (Langevin et al. 2022). Hydraulic mixing cells step such fractions forward in stream cells (Partington et al. 2011), then overland cells (Partington et al. 2013) and aquifer cells (Schilling et al. 2017; Nogueira et al. 2022). As documented, both run forward in time only (Table 1). For unit-concentration transport we read only the abstract of Bedekar et al. (2024). A forward run does not give the share of each cell's water that will end at the well.

Adjoint methods (Neupauer and Wilson 1999) and backward advective–dispersive transport (Frind et al. 2002) work backward in time. They give the probability that water at a well came from each location. Age and life-expectancy methods give mean travel times on the grid (Goode 1996; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006). These methods solve for concentrations in continuous transport, not water fractions on a flow model's time steps. Two further gaps remain. No method in Table 1 checks that a source-to-destination volume is the same traced backward or forward. We found no published comparison of a full-mixing delineation with particle-tracked areas on a large real model.

This paper extends the full-mixing method of FlowSource in open-source code for MODFLOW 6. The fractions are solved exactly, flow loops included (Section 2.4). By default each sink takes only its share of its cell's water (gross accounting, Section 2.3). A well is then credited with what it pumps. Net accounting is an option. Every trace comes with two budget checks that must hold exactly (Section 2.5). Traced volumes must add up to each destination's inflow. Backward and forward traces must give the same volume. The code reads MODFLOW 6 Groundwater Flow (GWF) output for every grid type and boundary package, multi-model simulations and package networks such as Streamflow Routing (SFR) reaches. Transient tracking carries tagged water through storage, as GWT does. Backward tracking in time (Section 2.8) gives the share of each cell's water at each time that will later leave through the well. A manager can then see how much pumped water was already stored in the aquifer.
