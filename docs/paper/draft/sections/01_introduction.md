# 1. Introduction

Protection zones around supply wells and springs restrict activities that could pollute the water they supply (Environment Agency 2019). Each zone is drawn from the well's contributing area, which is the land where water enters the aquifer and later reaches the well. Risk-based studies map this area for every realisation of a model ensemble, so that managers can choose the protection by their tolerance of risk (Fienen et al. 2022). This needs, for each model cell, the share of its water that reaches the well. These shares should agree with the model's water budget and be cheap enough to compute for every realisation.

Methods that compute these shares fall into four families. Particle tracking follows particles of water along flow paths. Full-mixing flow tracing splits each cell's mixed water among its outflows. Transport with tagged water follows the water from one source through time. Adjoint and age methods solve transport equations backward in time or for travel times.

Particle tracking (Pollock 1988, 2016) is the standard tool for contributing areas. Particles can also give the sources of a sink's water (Pryet et al. 2022). <!-- evidence: docs/paper/outline.md §1 (flux-based alternatives, particle source apportionment [PRY22]); references/SUMMARY.md §5.3 --> But particles need a rule at weak sinks. A weak sink is a cell whose sink terms, such as a well or a gaining stream, take only part of the cell's outflow. Because a particle cannot split, it must either stop there or pass on. If it stops, all its water is credited to the sink. If it passes, none is. The rule chosen changes the contributing area (Section 5.3). Backward particle tracking through transient flows can be awkward to set up and hard to interpret (Environment Agency 2019). <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, Appendix C4.3 ("awkward to arrange backward particle tracking through transient flow fields, and the results may be difficult to interpret") -->

Full-mixing flow tracing needs no such rule. Because the water in each cell is assumed fully mixed, a weak sink takes its share of the cell's water and passes the rest on. Backward from a destination, such as a well or a wellfield, full mixing gives each cell's backward fraction F. F is the share of the cell's water that ends at the destination. Forward from a source, such as recharge, it gives the forward fraction G. G is the share of a cell's water that came from the source. Each such solve is a trace. A contributing area is the set of cells where F exceeds a threshold, such as one half. F differs from the capture fraction of Leake et al. (2010) and Barlow et al. (2018). That fraction compares runs with and without pumping to find which budget terms supply the pumping. F comes from one run and says where the pumped water comes from.

Adjoint methods (Neupauer and Wilson 1999) and backward advective–dispersive transport (Frind et al. 2002) do work backward in time. They give the probability that water at a well came from each location. Groundwater age and life expectancy give mean travel times on the model grid (Goode 1996; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006). These methods solve a transport or adjoint equation of their own, rather than working from the flows a MODFLOW model has saved.

None of these methods checks that the water moving from a source to a destination is the same traced backward or forward. The Environment Agency (2019) recommends checking particle-tracked zones against FlowSource, but we found no published comparison of the two on a large real model. <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, Appendix C4.3 (MODPATH total capture zones "should be sense checked with the FlowSource outputs") --> This paper extends the method of FlowSource, which is not open source, in open-source code for MODFLOW 6. The code solves for the fractions exactly and deletes no flow in loops (Section 2.4). Gross accounting is the default. Net accounting is kept to reproduce FlowSource studies. Every trace comes with four budget checks that must hold exactly (Section 2.5). For example, traced volumes must add up to the destination's inflow.

The code reads MODFLOW 6 Groundwater Flow (GWF) output for every grid type, for multi-model simulations and for package networks such as Streamflow Routing (SFR) reaches. Transient tracking carries tagged water through storage from step to step, as GWT does. We add backward tracking in time, which maps the water that will reach the well within a stated time, as a time-limited protection zone requires (Section 2.8). On a model of 1.68 million cells, one trace takes 2–3 s (Section 5.1). <!-- NS Summary, §1, §4; checks_cells.csv (2.3 s), checks_sfr_network.csv (2.6 s); docs/neversink_study.md §1 (1,683,680 cells) --> Section 5 compares the method with particle tracking on a published regional model.
