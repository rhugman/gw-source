# 1. Introduction

Groundwater studies often ask where water comes from and where it goes. Which land feeds a supply well, a spring or a stream? How much of a well's water comes from a river, and how much was already stored in the aquifer? Where does the recharge under a polluted field end up? How old is the water when it arrives? Practitioners also need to know how the answers change through the year. They need to know how sure they can be of them. <!-- evidence: docs/paper/outline.md §3.1 (the practitioner's questions) -->

Methods that answer these questions from a groundwater flow model fall into four families. Particle tracking follows particles of water along flow paths. Transport with tagged water follows the water from one source through time. Adjoint and age methods solve their own transport equations. Full-mixing flow tracing splits each cell's mixed water among its outflows.

Particle tracking is the standard tool for mapping a well's contributing area. This is the land where water enters the aquifer and later reaches the well. In MODFLOW models it runs in MODPATH (Pollock 2016) or in the particle-tracking model built into MODFLOW 6 (PRT; Langevin et al. 2026). Particles can also give the sources of a sink's water (Pryet et al. 2022). <!-- evidence: docs/paper/outline.md §1 (flux-based alternatives, particle source apportionment [PRY22]); references/SUMMARY.md §5.3 --> But particles need a rule at weak sinks. Sink terms are the boundaries that remove water from a cell, such as a pumping well or a gaining stream. A weak sink is a cell whose sink terms take only part of the cell's outflow. Because a particle cannot split, it must either stop at a weak sink or pass on. If it stops, all its water is credited to the sink. If it passes, none is. The rule chosen changes the contributing area (Section 5). Backward particle tracking through transient flows can also be awkward to set up and hard to interpret (Environment Agency 2019). <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, Appendix C4.3 ("awkward to arrange backward particle tracking through transient flow fields, and the results may be difficult to interpret") -->

In transport with tagged water, the unit-concentration method gives the water from one source a concentration of 1 and all other water a concentration of 0. A cell's concentration is then the share of its water that came from that source (Bedekar et al. 2024). <!-- Bedekar p. 304: C = 1 for the source assessed, C = 0 for all other sources; the concentration distribution "signifies the fraction of water emanating from the particular source(s)". pp. 305–306: their examples use MODFLOW-2005 or MODFLOW-NWT with MT3D-USGS, not GWT; p. 305: "most solute transport codes" can be used --> In MODFLOW 6 this runs in the Groundwater Transport (GWT) Model (Langevin et al. 2022). Each source needs its own transport model. <!-- display/A.md, Table 1 row "Destinations or sources per run" (one tagged source per GWT model; several models per simulation); tutorials/10_unit_concentration (four GWT runs, one per source) --> As documented, the method steps forward in time only. <!-- Bedekar p. 306: well capture is read forward, from the concentration simulated at the pumping well over 25 years (Fig. 2); pp. 303–308 describe no backward-in-time or reversed-flow run --> So it gives the share of each cell's water that came from a source, but not the share that will later reach the well.

Adjoint and backward-transport methods give the probability that water at a well came from each location (Neupauer and Wilson 1999; Frind et al. 2002). Age methods give mean travel times (Goode 1996; Cornaton and Perrochet 2006). Both solve transport equations of their own, rather than working from the flows a MODFLOW model has saved.

Full-mixing flow tracing works from the flows a MODFLOW model has saved. It assumes that the water in each cell is fully mixed. A weak sink therefore needs no stop-or-pass rule. The sink takes its share of the cell's water. The rest passes on to the neighbouring cells. When the method works backward from a well, it gives each cell's backward fraction F. F is the share of the cell's water that ends at the well. A contributing area is the set of cells where F exceeds a chosen threshold, such as one half. When the method works forward from a source, such as recharge, it gives the share of each cell's water that came from that source.

Two full-mixing programs have been published. FlowSource (Foley and Black 2013; Black and Foley 2013) is used to draw source protection zones in England (Environment Agency 2019). These zones restrict activities that could pollute the water a supply well pumps. <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, Section 1 ("the use of FlowSource as a post-processing tool from the models to help produce the hydraulic capture zones"); Appendix C4.4 --> It credits a weak-sink well with all the water passing through its cell. We refer to this as net accounting. For transient models, FlowSource traces each time step as if its flows were steady, with no water carried from one step to the next. We refer to such a trace as a snapshot. <!-- references/docs/text/2019_EnvironmentAgency_SPZ_manual.txt, Appendix C4.4 ("For each stress period, FlowSource identifies how much of the water in each model cell is ultimately removed"); display/A.md, Table 1 rows "Accounting at weak sinks" and "Transient models"; si/A.md --> MODALL (Potter et al. 2008) computes full-mixing fractions for steady-state MODFLOW-88 models. <!-- Potter p. 336: "complete mix theory (Gelhar and Wilson 1974)"; step 1, "A steady-state MODFLOW-88 simulation is completed", files .cbc, .bas, .bcf, .hds; no transient use described anywhere in pp. 335–340 --> It credits a weak-sink well with only the water the well pumps. We refer to this as gross accounting. <!-- Potter Fig. 1, p. 336: at the sink cell CF = Q_sink / total inflow to the cell, drawn for a weak sink --> As published, neither program reads MODFLOW 6 output. The FlowSource source code is available for academic use on request (Foley and Black 2013). <!-- references/docs/text/2013_Foley_Black_FlowSource_DAG_algorithms.txt:452 and 2013_Black_Foley_FlowSource_application.txt:403 ("Source code (VB.NET) is available for Academic use on request"); MODALL is for MODFLOW-88 (Potter p. 336); both papers predate MODFLOW 6 (Langevin et al. 2017) --> A comparison of all these methods, with a source for each statement, is given separately ({SI: Table 1 notes and sources}).

This paper presents open-source code, `gwsource`, that extends the full-mixing method of FlowSource to the flows a MODFLOW 6 Groundwater Flow (GWF) model saves. It reads every MODFLOW 6 grid type and simulations made of several linked models. It also traces water through package networks, such as the stream reaches of the Streamflow Routing (SFR) package. <!-- src/gwsource (network.py, packages.py); display/A.md, Table 1 row "Grids and packages"; tutorials/04_grids, tutorials/07_networks_multimodel --> Gross accounting is the default. Net accounting is kept so that FlowSource studies can be reproduced. Every result comes with budget checks. For example, the traced volumes must add up to the well's pumping. <!-- previous/01_introduction.md:23 (gross default, budget checks); tutorials/01_quickstart (closure check) --> For transient models, the code carries tagged water through storage from step to step, as GWT does. We refer to this as transient tracking. It can also run backward in time, from the end of the simulation to its start. It then maps the area whose water reaches the well within a stated time. <!-- previous/02_methods.md:95 (backward tracking in time; time-limited area of Fig. 4d); tutorials/08_transient_tracking --> With a porosity, the code also gives the mean travel time of the water to the well. <!-- tutorials/06_travel_time (porosity as an explicit input; Eulerian mean travel time) --> The answers depend on model parameters that are never known exactly. Risk-based studies therefore run many versions of a model, each fitting the measurements about equally well (Fienen et al. 2022). We refer to this set as an ensemble and to each version as a realisation. A trace of a regional model of 1.68 million cells takes seconds. It can therefore be repeated for every realisation of an ensemble of hundreds (Section 5). <!-- docs/neversink_study.md Summary "Cost" (2 to 3 s for nine destination groups) and "Ensemble" (358 realisations, two traces each); §1 (1,683,680 cells) -->

Sections 2 and 3 describe the method and the software. Section 4 then works through ten small examples. Each answers one of the questions above and shows the output a practitioner would use. Where another approach can answer the same question, the example compares the two. Section 5 applies the code to a published regional model (Corson-Dosch et al. 2022; Fienen et al. 2022). Section 6 sets out the trade-offs of full mixing, including when particle tracking or transport is the better choice.
