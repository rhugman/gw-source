## 4.6 How old is the water reaching the well?

Protection zones are often drawn by travel time as well as by area. A spill 50 days upgradient of a well calls for a different response from a spill 50 years upgradient. This example adds the mean travel time of Section 2.6 to the backward trace of the shared model (Section 4). The only new input is a porosity of 0.3. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb Settings (POROSITY 0.3) and §1 (build_quickstart, the same backward trace) --> In a steady-state model every travel time is proportional to porosity, so a range of porosities needs no new run. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §2 "Times scale with porosity" (ratio 0.666667 for n = 0.2 against 0.3, in every cell) -->

The mean travel time of all the water reaching the well is 46 years. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §5 (volume / pumping rate 16,791.3 d = 46.0 yr) --> River water reaches the well in about 2 years, because the losing river cells lie next to it. Recharge takes 42 years on average. Inflow from the west edge takes 108 years, because it enters at the far end of the contributing area. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §5, source table (to the well: river 2.2, recharge 41.7, west edge 108.4 yr) --> A time-of-travel zone holds the cells with F > 0.5 whose mean travel time is shorter than a stated limit (Fig. 7a). The 5-year zone covers 0.098 km² and the 25-year zone 0.455 km². <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §4 zone table (39 and 182 cells; the notebook writes F ≥ 0.5, which selects the same cells as F > 0.5 on this model, outline §3.4 item 4.1) --> Water spends about a month in the well cell, so 50 m cells cannot draw a 50-day zone. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §2 (residence time in the well cell 32.2 d) and §4 (50 d: 1 cell) -->

Particle tracking with PRT gives a purely advective answer on the same flows. Each particle carries a share of a source term's flow as its weight. The particles are weight-losing. At each sink they pass, they lose the share of their weight that the sink takes from the cell, as full mixing does. A particle stops when it enters the well cell, so the full-mixing times are also taken to the well cell. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6 (track_particles, thin_weak_sinks=True default; 18,208 particles; captured particle flow 799.9 of 800.0 m³/d) --> Over all the water, the mean particle time is 45.7 years, 0.5 % below the full-mixing time of 45.9 years. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6 "Overall means" (PRT 45.7, full mixing to the well cell 45.9 yr, −0.5 %) --> This agreement is not an independent test. In both methods, the mean travel time of all the water is close to the volume of water that will end at the well divided by the pumping rate. That volume is nearly the same in both. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §5 (volume identity, relative error 0.0) and the text after "Overall means" -->

The comparison cell by cell is the informative one (Fig. 7b). In the 456 cells with F > 0.5, the median ratio of particle time to full-mixing time is 0.96. In the 676 edge cells with F < 0.5, it is 5.7. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §6 "Cell by cell" (core F ≥ 0.5: 456 cells, median 0.96; edge F < 0.5: 676 cells, median 5.72) --> A particle from an edge cell follows one slow path around the edge of the zone. Full mixing averages over all the paths that the cell's water takes. The particle time-of-travel zone is 26 % larger at 5 years and 2.2 % larger at 25 years. <!-- evidence: tutorials/06_travel_time/06_travel_time.ipynb §7 zone table (5 yr: 0.122 against 0.098 km², +0.256; 25 yr: 0.465 against 0.455 km², +0.022) --> A mean travel time is not the first arrival of a contaminant, so short time-of-travel zones still need particles on a grid refined near the well.

## 4.7 What if the water passes through streams, drains, wells or other models?

In many models water leaves the aquifer cells and comes back. A drain may pass its water to a stream through the water mover (MVR). The stream may then lose that water to the aquifer near a well. A trace on the aquifer cells alone stops at the stream. It reports the leakage as a source with no history. This example shows what changes when stream reaches and wells are traced as cells of their own (Section 3.2).

The river of the shared model becomes a stream of the SFR package, with one reach per row, flowing from north to south. The first reach receives 300 m³/d from upstream. Two drains lie 800 m west of the stream. They pass 80 % and all of their water to the first reach through the mover. The upper reaches lose water to the aquifer. The reaches below the well gain water from it. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §1; tutorials/herebedragons.py build_stream_mover, STREAM_DRAINS (rows 9 and 23, column 15, 1-based), DRAIN_TO_STREAM (0.8, 1.0); stream in column 31 --> 

On the aquifer cells alone, 32 % of the well's water is stream leakage (Fig. 8a). <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §2 (SFR 255.2 m³/d, share 0.319) --> With the reaches traced as cells, that water splits into 129 m³/d of upstream stream inflow and 126 m³/d of recharge. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §3 "The well's water by original source" (SFR:EXT-INFLOW 129.4; RCH 670.6 against 544.8, +125.8 m³/d) --> That recharge reached the stream through the drains and the mover. The land drained by each drain therefore joins the contributing area (Fig. 8b). Of the 800 m³/d pumped, 188 m³/d passed through the mover. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §3 "Trace again" (block solver, one flow loop through 711 nodes) and "The route, by numbers" (188.3 m³/d through the mover, 23.5 %) --> The F of each reach shows which part of the stream feeds the well (Fig. 8c). A multi-aquifer well (MAW) can carry water between layers in a similar way. A three-layer version of the shared model has an aquitard as its middle layer. The supply well draws from the bottom layer. A MAW screened in all three layers pumps 10 m³/d. Its borehole takes 24.3 m³/d from the upper two layers and passes 14.3 m³/d into the bottom layer. On the cells alone, those 14.3 m³/d of the supply well's water have no origin. With the borehole traced as a cell, they are recharge that fell near the MAW. <!-- evidence: tutorials/07_networks_multimodel/07_networks_multimodel.ipynb §4 (vertical conductivity 1 % of horizontal; MAW −23.21, −1.13, +14.34 m³/d; borehole 24.34 m³/d; MAW 14.34 → RCH 296.25 to 310.59 m³/d) -->

## 4.8 How much of the pumped water arrived since the start?

Snapshots of the seasonal model (Section 4.5) cannot say where the water released from storage came from. They also have no travel lag. A manager may ask how much of the water pumped over the next decades is water already in the aquifer today. Forward transient tracking answers this (Section 2.7). Backward tracking in time also says which of today's water will reach the well within 30 years.

The seasonal model of Section 4.5 runs here for 30 years, with one time step per season. The run starts with a 1-day steady-state period in which the well already pumps. Initial water is therefore not water from before pumping began. Porosity is 0.3, against a specific yield of 0.2. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb Settings and §1 (N_YEARS 30, STEPS_PER_SEASON 1, POROSITY 0.3; 121 time steps; specific yield 0.2) --> Each 91-day flow step is split into four transport sub-steps. Sub-steps shorten the transport step without changing the flow model. Longer transport steps bring tagged water to the well too early. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb Settings (TRANSPORT_SUBSTEPS 4) and §9 (first-step recharge share 1.85 times the 16-sub-step value with one step, 1.17 times with four) -->

Snapshots give the well's water on average as 60 % recharge, 14 % river leakage, 12 % west-edge inflow and 15 % storage release (Fig. 9a). <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3 (snapshots, time-weighted mean: recharge 0.60, river 0.14, west edge 0.12, storage 0.15) --> In the last season of year 30, the well's water is 52 % initial water, 33 % recharge and 16 % river water (Fig. 9b). <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3 table (year 30: initial 0.516, recharge 0.329, river 0.155, west edge 0.0) --> Of all the water pumped in the first 10 years, 81 % was initial water. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §4 table (initial water, all pumped so far: 0.976, 0.813 and 0.677 after 1, 10 and 30 years) --> New recharge mixes into a large volume of older water, because a column of this aquifer holds about 64 years of recharge. <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §3 (water column 11.4 m, recharge 0.18 m/yr, 64 years) --> With one layer, the model cannot hold young water above old water, so a layered model may give other shares.

Backward tracking in time splits the water in each cell at the start into the fractions that the well pumps within 30 years, that the river takes and that are still stored (Fig. 9d–f). The area whose water reaches the well within 30 years stops well short of the west edge. The steady-state F area reaches it (Fig. 9c). <!-- evidence: tutorials/08_transient_tracking/08_transient_tracking.ipynb §5 (fractions add up to 1.0000 over active cells; backward split RIV 0.138, RCH 0.185, initial 0.677 of 8,766,800 m³) -->
