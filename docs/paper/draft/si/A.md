### Notes on the cells

- **What is solved, and how; this formulation.** One set of equations for the backward fraction F and one for the forward fraction G. Without flow loops the equations are solved cell by cell in flow order; with flow loops, loop by loop in flow order. Net accounting solves one destination group at a time. In transient tracking, each time step (or sub-step, when the water volumes change within a flow step) is one set of linear equations, implicit in time, solved for all tagged groups at once.
- **What is solved, and how; GWT unit concentration.** Langevin et al. (2022): GWT "does not have an option to calculate steady-state transport solutions"; steady state is reached by running transient steps until the concentrations stabilise. The GWT Model needs the storage (MST) package: without it, MODFLOW 6 (6.9.0.dev0) stops at input with "Mass storage and transfer (MST6) package not specified". With MST porosity set to 0, a use not described in the parts of Langevin et al. (2022) read, one transport step on the flows of test model 1 gave the steady-state unit concentration equal to G to 9.5 × 10⁻¹³ (recharge tagged) and 9.7 × 10⁻¹³ (river tagged), with no warning in the list file (our test). <!-- table1_methods.md, Sources, GWT unit concentration, 'Solved, how'; tests/models/gwt.py build_gwt_fmi -->
- **What is solved, and how; hydraulic mixing cells.** The fractions at each step are computed from the previous step's fractions and the volumes moved over the step; no system of equations is solved. The update is stable only if the volume entering or leaving a cell over a step does not exceed the cell's storage; small cells and long steps cause oscillations. Later versions: Partington et al. (2012, Section 2.2) apply the method of Partington et al. (2011) unchanged, built into a version of HydroGeoSphere. Partington et al. (2013, Section 3.3) add overland-flow fractions, sub-time-steps and cell resets. The HMC ratio, the outflow over a step divided by the cell's storage at the start of the step (Eq. 2), must stay below 1; where it exceeds 1 in any cell, each flow step is split into s = [max. HMC ratio] + 1 sub-steps, and the fluxes and storage changes are split among them (Eqs 3–9). A cell that meets any of five criteria, such as an HMC ratio above 10⁴ or |1 − Σf| > 0.5, is reset: its fractions are set to 0 and its water is assigned to a tracked "reset" fraction (Section 3.3.3). The 2013 mixing cells are still surface cells only, stream and overland cells (Section 3.3.1); tracking in the subsurface is recommended as future work (Section 6, para. 73). <!-- table1_methods.md, footnote 3 and Sources, Hydraulic mixing cells; Partington et al. 2013 Eqs 2–9, §3.3.3 --> Fractions in aquifer cells came later. Schilling et al. (2017, Section 2.4.2.1) run the mixing cells on a HydroGeoSphere model of the alluvial river–aquifer system around a drinking-water wellfield in the Emme valley, Switzerland (Section 2.2). Water entering through each boundary condition is tagged and its fraction tracked "in each of the model cells"; Figure 5 maps the river-water fraction in the aquifer. They give no update equation, stability limit or reset criterion. Numerical dispersion is kept small with a fine grid and a sub-time-stepping scheme that sets the step size automatically; they cite Partington et al. (2011) for it, although in our reading sub-steps first appear in Partington et al. (2013). Fractions are computed for steady-state and transient runs (Section 2.4.1). The river-water fraction in the pumped water is compared with fractions from noble-gas recharge temperatures and helium isotope ratios (Eqs 8–9; Section 3.4, Fig. 8) and used to choose the riverbed conductivity and aquifer porosity; no water-balance check of the fractions and no transport run are reported. Nogueira et al. (2022, Sect. 2.2, Eq. 1) track fractions in every model cell, surface and subsurface, of a riparian zone. All versions read step forward in time only.
- **Mixing in a cell; GWT unit concentration.** GWT also offers central-in-space and TVD advection, dispersion and reactions (Langevin et al. 2022). With upstream weighting and no dispersion its steady-state limit equals G (Section 4.1; Fig. 2). Bedekar et al. (2024) ran their examples in MT3D-USGS and do not name the advection scheme. They left mechanical dispersion out of their synthetic example and dispersion and diffusion out of their field example. They warn that numerical dispersion, the spreading added by the numerical scheme, changes the timing of source contributions. <!-- Bedekar pp. 305–306 (MT3D-USGS; no dispersion in either example); p. 305 and p. 307 (numerical dispersion, oscillations and mass balance errors) -->
- **Mixing in a cell; hydraulic mixing cells.** After the modified mixing cell of Campana and Simpson (1984): water leaving a cell over a step carries the previous step's mix, and water entering stays until the next step.
- **Accounting at weak sinks; FlowSource.** Foley and Black (2013) define all water starting in or passing through a destination cell as reaching the destination, so a weak-sink well is credited with all the water passing through its cell.
- **Flow loops; this formulation.** A closed recirculation loop has no outflow to any sink or to a cell outside the loop; it is given F = 0 and reported. With net accounting, the smallest-flow connection in each loop can instead be deleted, as in FlowSource (`remove_cycles`).
- **Outputs; FlowSource.** Volume through, volume from (by budget term) and fraction from; statistics over time steps.
- **Outputs; this formulation.** Also boundary-control maps (the fractions to all sinks, which add up to 1 in every cell), mean travel time, statistics over steps and, in transient tracking, the fractions leaving through other sinks and still stored.
- **Outputs; hydraulic mixing cells.** K flow components: groundwater, unsaturated flow, overland flow, preferential flow and direct rainfall. Their fractions are updated together and sum to 1 in each cell. Initial fractions are assumed, and a dummy fraction holds the initial water until it is flushed out.
- **Backward in time; GWT unit concentration.** Langevin et al. (2022) document no backward-in-time option. A backward steady-state run would need a flow field with reversed flows and swapped sources and sinks: possible, but awkward. Potter et al. (2008) made such a run in MT3DMS for one well in uniform flow, with the flow reversed and the well turned into an injection well of concentration 1. <!-- Potter p. 338: "the regional flow direction was reversed and extraction well was converted to an injection well with a constant dimensionless concentration of 1.0"; dispersivity 0 --> Bedekar et al. (2024) list well capture analysis among the uses. In their example the share of each source at the well is read forward in time, from the concentration simulated there. They describe no backward-in-time or reversed-flow run. <!-- Bedekar p. 303 (uses); p. 306 and Fig. 2 (concentrations monitored at the pumping well over 25 years); pp. 303–308 (no backward run) -->
- **Built-in water-budget checks; this formulation.** Also a report of closed recirculation loops. In transient tracking: the forward–backward check in time and the water and tagged-water balances.
- **Built-in water-budget checks; GWT unit concentration.** With unit concentration the solute mass is the tagged water volume, so the GWT mass budget, by term and step, is a tagged-water balance. The GWT budget file holds the solute mass flows of each cell by term (intercell, storage, source and sink mixing, advanced packages), and the list file reports the mass budget of the whole model with its percent discrepancy at each saved step (both seen in our runs). GWT has no backward solve, so the backward closure, forward–backward and boundary-control checks of Section 2.5 have no GWT counterpart.
- **Built-in water-budget checks; hydraulic mixing cells.** Relative error e = 1 − Σf, from the fractions summing to 1. The global volume error compares the groundwater volume in the stream cells at the end with the exfiltration summed over the run, from the model's water balance; it holds only on reaches that gain all along. It compares a traced volume with a water-budget total, close to our closure check.
- **Grids and packages; this formulation.** DIS, DISV and DISU grids; GWF-GWF exchanges; SFR reaches, lakes, MAW wells and UZF cells added as extra cells and linked by MVR.
- **Grids and packages; hydraulic mixing cells.** Stream cells of the HydroGeoSphere surface domain. Partington et al. (2011) say the method can be implemented in any code that reports surface–subsurface exchange in a spatially distributed way.
- **MODALL, all rows.** MODALL (Potter et al. 2008) reads the saved cell-by-cell flows of a steady-state MODFLOW-88 model and assumes full mixing in each cell (p. 336). For each extraction well it computes the capture fraction. This is the share of each cell's water that the well takes in the end, the backward fraction F of Section 2.2 (p. 336). The calculation starts at the well cell. There the fraction is the well's pumping divided by the cell's total inflow (Fig. 1, p. 336). A weak-sink well is thus credited with what it pumps, which is gross accounting (Section 2.3). The other cells follow one at a time, working outward from the well. Each cell's fraction is the sum of its outflows to its neighbours, each weighted by that neighbour's fraction, divided by its inflow (Fig. 1). In our reading this is one sweep through the cells, not an iteration or a linear solve. The paper does not say how the cells are put in order or what happens where water circulates in a flow loop. Foley and Black (2013) state that MODALL does not use graphs, on which the flow-order sort of FlowSource rests. Black and Foley (2013) report, from a personal communication, that MODALL did not delineate the capture areas on a model with flow loops. They attribute this to the flow model, not to the MODALL method. Before the sweep, a first pass limits the calculation to the capture envelope. This is the set of cells with some flow from a source or to a sink. The other cells are treated as no-flow cells (p. 336). One output file is written per well. A further file adds them up for the whole system (p. 336). The paper says MODALL can also give the share of each cell's water that came from a source. It names recharge, rivers and drains, besides wells, as sources or sinks (pp. 335–336, 338). The worked calculation covers extraction wells only. No water-budget check is described. For one well in uniform flow, the contour where the fraction is 0.5 matched the capture zone of an analytical solution and of MODPATH (pp. 337–338). MT3DMS run on the reversed flows gave the same contours (p. 338). The fractions spread more widely on coarser grids (p. 338). McLane and Cecan (2009) add that this spread is not hydrodynamic dispersion and that the fractions are not a probability of capture. No run times are given. The authors offer the tool free of charge for unrestricted use (p. 336). The paper does not say whether the source code is available.

### FlowSource

- **Solved, how:** Foley and Black (2013) Method and Evaluation: cells sorted in flow order, then one arithmetic pass; earlier versions used recursive scans. Each time step processed on its own; Foley and Black (2013) Introduction.
- **Mixing:** Foley and Black (2013) Description of Calculation (divergent cells split their water in proportion to outflow); stated as full mixing in Chowdhury (2016) Section 2.4.3.
- **Accounting:** Foley and Black (2013) Description of Calculation: all water starting in or passing through a destination cell reaches the destination (cell A, 100 %); volume from uses each cell's excess of outflow over inflow. The outflows of destination cells are detached so that nothing downstream is counted; Foley and Black (2013) Method.
- **Flow loops:** Foley and Black (2013) Method (the lowest-volume connection is removed; which one to remove is called an open research question) and Evaluation; Black and Foley (2013) Introduction to Graphs ("lowest flow volume cell connection(s)"). Dated 2013 because we found no published description of later versions.
- **Outputs:** Foley and Black (2013) Introduction and Figs 1–4, 6–8 (volume and fraction through, volume and fraction from; volume from by recharge, storage and boundary term if other budget records are read); Black and Foley (2013) Introduction (min, max, mean, ASN, probability within range; travel time from forward particles based on MODPATH 6).
- **Transient:** Foley and Black (2013) Introduction and Black and Foley (2013) Introduction and Problem Description: each time step a "time-instant steady state" (Rushton 2003); particles for travel time tracked through each step's steady-state flows; Black and Foley (2013) Introduction.
- **Backward in time:** none in Foley and Black (2013) or Black and Foley (2013); it follows from the snapshot treatment.
- **Per solve:** Foley and Black (2013) Evaluation and Black and Foley (2013) river-catchment and boundary-control examples: up to about 150,000 destination cells processed as one group in one calculation; the four boundary-control maps (Figs 18–21) were "processed individually" and sum to 1 in every cell.
- **Checks:** Foley and Black (2013) Method (flow loops reported to the user as a sign of a model flaw). No closure or other budget check is described.
- **Grids and packages:** Foley and Black (2013) Abstract and Introduction (MODFLOW-96 and MODFLOW-2005 CBC files; method said to apply to MODFLOW-USG and finite elements); Black and Foley (2013) (MODFLOW-96). MODFLOW 6 exchanges, MVR and package networks are not mentioned in either paper. <!-- references/SUMMARY.md §2.9, §8 -->
- **Open implementation:** Foley and Black (2013) and Black and Foley (2013) end matter (available on request; VB.NET source for academic use on request); the vendor's web pages (as listed on 2026-10-01) (machine-locked, dongle and shared licences sold).

- **Solved, how:** Langevin et al. (2022) abstract (implicit, iterative methods; the quoted words on steady state) and p. 2–1 (the cell balances form a system of linear equations); p. 1–7, items 4 and 10 (central, upstream or TVD advection; "no option to automatically run the GWT Model to steady state using a single time step"; steady state found by running transient until concentrations stabilise). Note on GWT steady state: our runs of a GWT model on the saved flows of test model 1 (upstream advection, no dispersion, flow-imbalance correction on; recharge or river tagged; GWT outer/inner DVCLOSE 1 × 10⁻¹²/1 × 10⁻¹³, RCLOSE 1 × 10⁻⁹) with MODFLOW 6 6.9.0.dev0. <!-- tests/models/gwt.py build_gwt_fmi; run in a scratch folder outside the repository --> With MST left out the run stops at input. - **Outputs:** Bedekar et al. ### Hydraulic mixing cells

All cells rest on the Partington et al. (2011) manuscript, read in full. The column describes Partington et al. (2011) itself, in which the mixing cells are stream cells of the surface domain only (Section 3.1, "a stream cell i"; Sections 3.2–3.3, pairs of adjacent HydroGeoSphere grid points across the stream). Later versions, from the full texts of Partington et al. (2012, 2013), Schilling et al. (2017) and Nogueira et al. (2022), are in the note on hydraulic mixing cells (What is solved, and how).

- **Solved, how:** Section 3.1, Eqs 8–9: the fraction of component k in cell i at step N from the cell's storage at N − 1 and N, the outflow volume over the step at the cell's own fraction at N − 1 and the inflow volumes at the neighbours' fractions at N − 1; no system of equations. Stability (Section 3.1, after Eq. 9; Section 4): the volume entering or leaving a cell over a step must not exceed its storage; small cells and long steps cause oscillations, so suitable time steps are needed. Volumes over each step come from the flow model's fluxes at the end of the step (Eq. 11); no sub-steps.
- **Mixing:** Sections 3 and 4 (based on the modified mixing cell of Campana and Simpson 1984; "the modified mixing rule has to be used" unless mixing in the river is simulated); Section 3.2 (outflow over a step at the previous step's fractions; water entering stays until the next step); Section 3.1, Eqs 9–10 (Eq. 9 tends to the perfectly mixed cell, Eq. 10, as the step goes to 0).
- **Accounting:** Section 3.2 and Eq. 9: every outflow of the cell, downstream or to the subsurface, uses the cell's previous-step fractions. That this is gross accounting in effect is our reading; Partington et al. (2011) does not discuss weak sinks.
- **Flow loops:** not discussed. Sections 3 and 4: constant stream width and fixed stream flow direction assumed in the coding.
- **Outputs:** Section 3.1, Eqs 5–7 (K components: groundwater, unsaturated flow, overland flow, preferential flow and direct rainfall; fractions sum to 1); Section 3.2 (a "dummy (or undefined) fraction" for the initial water until the stream water is turned over). Applied as the groundwater component of streamflow at each stream cell (Section 3.3.2, Figs 11–12).
- **Transient:** Section 3.1, Eqs 4–6 (cell volumes and component volumes carried from step N − 1 to N); Section 3.2 (initial fractions are subjective; the dummy fraction falls to zero once the stream water is turned over).
- **Backward in time:** none; the method steps forward from initial fractions (Sections 3.1–3.2).
- **Per solve:** Eqs 5–8 (all K components updated together).
- **Checks:** Section 3.3.1, Eq. 13 (relative error e from the sum of fractions; maximum 1.5 × 10⁻³ % in test case 1, 8.7 × 10⁻³ % in test case 2) and Eq. 14 (global volume error: the summed groundwater volume in the stream cells at the end against the summed exfiltration over the run from the model's water balance; "can only be used along completely gaining sections" with no losses; Table 1 of Partington et al. (2011)). The global volume error compares a traced volume with a water-budget total, as our closure check does.
- **Grids and packages:** Section 3.2 (HydroGeoSphere output: surface water depth, contributing area, surface–subsurface exchange flux, and upstream and downstream fluxes); Sections 1 and 3 (can be implemented in any code that reports surface–subsurface exchange in a spatially distributed way); Section 3.1 (any FD, FV or FE scheme if the cell water balance is mass conservative).
- **Open implementation:** Section 3.2 ("coded in Visual Basic for Excel and is used as a post-processing tool on HGS outputs"); no statement on availability.

### MODALL

Potter et al. (2008), read in full (pp. 335–340). MODALL interfaces with MODFLOW (p. 335). Step 1 runs a steady-state MODFLOW-88 model. MODALL then reads its .cbc, .bas, .bcf and .hds files, with a user-made .all file of the injection and extraction rates (p. 336). No transient use is described. The flow allocation rests on complete mix theory (p. 336). Step 2, the first pass, finds the capture envelope, the cells with some flow from a source or to a sink. The other cells get a "synthetic no-flow boundary condition" (p. 336). Step 3, the second pass, starts at the sink cell. There the capture fraction CF is the sink's outflow divided by the cell's total inflow. The other cells are computed "explicitly, working from the sink outward", each as the sum of CF times outflow over its downstream neighbours divided by its inflow (p. 336, Fig. 1). Fig. 1 is drawn for a weak sink, so the sink cell's CF is below 1. In our terms this is gross accounting. The paper does not say how the cells are ordered for the sweep. It does not mention flow loops, circulating flow or a water-budget check. One head-save file is written per well, NODES001.0 to NODES00n.0, with values from 0 to 1. Step 4 adds them by superposition into NODES000.T for the whole system (p. 336). The abstract and p. 336 describe fractions from a source as well as to a sink. P. 338 names drains and rivers as sinks and recharge and rivers as sources. The worked steps cover extraction wells only. Grids: a finite-difference grid. P. 338 says MODALL "can compute three-dimensional complex systems". Both examples have one layer, the field model 307 rows by 487 columns (p. 338). Verification: one well with Q = 5.5 m³/d in a confined aquifer with T = 18.6 m²/d and gradient 0.002. The capture envelopes of an analytical solution (Eq. 1) and of MODPATH match the CF = 0.5 contour (Fig. 2a, pp. 337–338). With the flow reversed and the well made an injection well of concentration 1.0 and dispersivity 0, MT3DMS contours are "exactly equivalent" to the CF contours (Fig. 2b, p. 338). The spread is attributed to numerical dispersion. Finer cells shrink the area of uncertain capture while the 0.5 contour stays essentially the same (p. 338). Field use: a 5000 m TCE plume, more than 50 extraction and reinjection wells, total pumping cut from 3300 to 2000 m³/d (p. 338). Run time: no figures. MODALL is said to be much more efficient than solute transport codes because it solves no transport equations (p. 338). Availability: developed at ARCADIS from the early 1990s and first presented at a conference in 2006 (p. 336). The tool is free of charge for unrestricted use from the authors, with no warranty (p. 336). The paper says nothing of the source code or a licence. Secondary accounts follow. Chowdhury (2016) Sections 2.3.3 (pp. 23–24) and 2.4: steady-state MODFLOW only; outputs equal the fraction through of FlowSource. Chowdhury (2016) p. 103: full mixing, numerical dispersion on coarse grids (citing McLane and Cecan 2009), a simpler calculation than FlowSource, without its flow-order sort. McLane and Cecan (2009) p. 172 and Fig. 1 (p. 173): quoting Potter et al., MODALL works from the MODFLOW cell-by-cell flow terms to "determine the percentage of flow in a particular cell that has either originated from a given source or flows to a specific sink"; their tests found that it "calculates accurately and efficiently", with no detail; on the simple example of Potter et al. at grid spacings of 25, 50, 100 and 200 ft, "the larger the grid spacing, the broader the spread" of the fractions, which "is not related in any way to mass transport hydrodynamic dispersion"; fractions are plotted outside the capture zone (fig. 2a of Potter et al.) and are not a probability of capture; Potter et al. are quoted as finding MT3DMS concentration contours with zero dispersivity "exactly equivalent" to the MODALL fractions. They do not use the words numerical dispersion, and do not describe the solution method, weak sinks, flow loops, transient use or how many sources or sinks a run handles. Foley and Black (2013) Introduction: MODALL capture fractions are "equivalent" to the fractional volume through of FlowSource. MODALL "does not apply graphs" and was used to validate FlowSource. In our reading of Potter et al. (Fig. 1), the two differ at weak-sink cells and upstream of them, where FlowSource credits the destination with all the water passing through. Black and Foley (2013) Problem Description: on a model with flow loops MODALL did not delineate the capture areas (personal communication cited there), "a deficiency with the underlying groundwater model and not the MODALL method". Tosco et al. (2010, p. 46) list MODALL among tools for choosing wells and pumping rates, without detail. Bedekar et al. (2024, p. 304) describe MODALL as computing cell-by-cell flow terms in MODFLOW for capture zones of pump-and-treat systems, tied to MODFLOW output and steady-state flows, so that it gives no arrival times. The secondary accounts agree with the full text on steady state, full mixing and the dependence on grid spacing. Only Black and Foley (2013) say anything on flow loops.
